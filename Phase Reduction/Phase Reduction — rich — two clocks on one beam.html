Two clocks on one beam

In 1665 Huygens, ill in bed, watched two of his pendulum clocks on a shared support keep returning to the same pattern however they were started or disturbed, an "odd kind of sympathy"; the pattern was the two swinging opposite ways. The page reads this as the smallest case of its bridge: two loops, coupled through the beam, falling into a phase-locked shadow. Here are two clocks you can hear, tick on the way right and a lower tock on the way left, one clock in each ear. Nothing plays until you press a sound button.

Step 1 of 4 · one clock, its own swing

A clock is a loop it falls back onto

A pendulum alone would swing ever smaller. The escapement feeds it a little each swing, more when the swing is small and less when it is wide, so the clock settles to a swing of its own whatever you start it at. That settled swing is its loop. Each tick is louder the faster the bob passes the middle, so you can hear it settle.

Step 2 of 4 · two clocks, nothing between them

On a stone wall, they slide past each other for ever

Two clocks, the right one a fraction fast, on a wall that does not move. Each keeps its own time. Started together, their ticks drift apart, the left tick and the right tock meet, and they drift on until they are together again. The gap between them slides one whole cycle every 1 ÷ δ swings and never stops.

Step 3 of 4 · Huygens's beam

On a beam that can sway, they settle together

Hang the same two clocks, the right one 0.3% fast, from a beam that can sway a little and damps its own swaying. Every swing of each bob shoves the beam, and the beam's sway shoves both bobs back. Started nearly together, the clocks slide apart and then hold, nearly opposite: the left tick lands with the right tock. Swinging nearly opposite, their shoves on the beam mostly cancel, and the beam's sway falls to about a tenth.

Step 4 of 4 · the test

The shadow, and what sets the gap it locks at

The page says the two clocks phase-reduce to a two-body Kuramoto system that locks antiphase, and that the PRC of the clock's escapement sets the locked gap. Build the shadow: the clock's phase response (gold, from Floquet, one clock alone on a wall), times the push the beam carries from one clock to the other (blue), averaged round a cycle, gives the pull; where the part of the pull that draws them falls through zero, the shadow settles. Then hang the real clocks, from four different starting gaps, and see where they settle. The escapement is the same under all three beams.

TryStep through the three beams with the escapement untouched: antiphase, in phase, and a gap of about 140° that neither the page nor Huygens names. Then keep the board that rolls freely and turn the escapement down to 0.6: the shadow swings back to antiphase, and the clocks themselves go two ways, one start holding in phase where the shadow says it cannot.
