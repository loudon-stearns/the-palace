The kick that stays

An oscillator that keeps falling back onto its loop forgets most of what you do to it. Kick it, and the part of the kick that pushed it off the loop dies away within a few trips. The part that pushed it along the loop never dies: it is now a little ahead, or a little behind, for good. Read that leftover at every point of the loop and you have the phase response curve. Nothing plays until you press a sound button; headphones help.

Step 1 of 4 · one loop, one kick

Off the loop it dies; along the loop it stays

The gold loop is where the oscillator settles. Two copies run round it together; one of them gets a kick, a push straight up in y. Follow the blue one: it is thrown off the loop, falls back onto it, and ends up ahead of or behind the white twin. Each trip round, the distance off the loop is multiplied by the same small number. The distance along it is multiplied by 1. Those two numbers are the loop's Floquet multipliers.

In the sound, the kicked copy is in your right ear and its twin in your left; after the kick, a lead of a few tenths of a radian at 220 Hz usually pulls the sound toward the ear that is ahead, and it stays there.

Step 2 of 4 · a kick at every phase

Where the kick lands decides what it leaves

The same kick, landing at different moments of the cycle, leaves different marks. Early in one stretch it pushes the oscillator ahead; in another it holds it back; at a few points it does almost nothing. Kick, wait for the loop to swallow the rest, and read how far ahead or behind it ended, per unit of kick. Plot that against where the kick landed. Do it at every phase and a curve draws itself.

Step 3 of 4 · the loop's shape sets the curve's

A different loop, a different curve

Change the loop and the curve changes with it. On the nearly round loop the curve is close to a plain sine: over half the cycle a push up in y moves the oscillator ahead, over the other half it holds it back, and the change between is smooth. On the relaxation loop, which crawls along two slow stretches and jumps between them, the curve sits nearly level along each slow stretch and swings over sharply near the jumps. This is what makes the curve something to design: shape the loop and you shape how it answers a push.

(how x moves) = y · (how y moves) = μ × (one minus x squared) × y, which feeds the swing while it is small and brakes it when it is wide, minus x, which pulls it back to the middle. μ sets how unevenly the loop is traced.

TryListen to each loop: the round one is close to a pure tone, the relaxation one buzzes. The buzz and the sharp curve are the same fact, heard and measured.

Step 4 of 4 · the test

The curve your kicks drew is Floquet data

The page says the phase response curve is derived from the left eigenvector of the monodromy at multiplier 1: take one trip round the loop as a matrix, find the direction it leaves alone, and read it at each phase. The gold line is that eigenvector, computed from the loop's equations with no kicking at all. The dots are kicks. If the page is right, the dots fall on the line, and fall closer the smaller the kick, because a finite kick also bends the loop a little and an infinitesimal one does not.
