The 2.5D paper stack · the two contraries

Reveal the stack, then break it

The same plate as three sheets of paper: the sky, the ridge, and the walker on it, each cut along its own ink. Four steps: two sheets with air between, the stack revealed, the stack flat again under a moving camera, and last the break, tried three ways.

loading the sheets…

1 · Two sheets, air between

The sky and the ridge (the walker rides on it, for now), cut apart and laid one over the other. With no air and no turn they are one picture. Give them air, then turn them, or drag across the stack.

At zero air the turn moves the picture as one piece of paper; with air, the ridge slides across the sky and the gap shows. That sliding is all the depth a stack has.

2 · Reveal the stack

Now the walker is a third sheet, and the paper shows: each sheet throws a shadow on the one behind, and the stack sways a little on its own. The device is part of the look.

Behind the walker and the ridge is the fill from the sheet maker, a smooth guess at the sky. Turn far enough and you see it; a stack that shows itself has to be cut with that in mind.

3 · Flat again

The same three sheets, turned square to us inside a panel. The camera drifts sideways and each sheet slides by its own depth: the multiplane move. It still reads as a flat drawing.

Each sheet moves by the camera's step times its depth: the sky 0.3, the ridge 0.5, the walker 0.55. Nothing turns. The walker's feet are cut inside the ridge's black, so the small slide between them doesn't show.

4 · Break the stack

The walker tears loose: he leaves his plane, turns, and comes out past the panel. Try it three ways. Out of the flat stack of step 3; out of a revealed stack, its paper and air showing but still; and out of a revealed stack that turns, the sway easing in from the click. The page holds reveal and break as contraries, both true; this step is one way to feel where they meet.

Turn is the cue that says “3D” here, so the measure is how many degrees of it the eye has seen from the click to the break, against the degrees in the break itself. Both numbers are this piece's choices: a sway of ±14° and a break of 38°. A smaller sway would give a bigger contrast.
