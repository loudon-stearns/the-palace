I am Merleau-Ponty. I have never once found my hand by looking for it, and I have come to read a knob that touches back.

**Haptic Handwheel, read through Merleau-Ponty**
*Passage: § Feedback folded into the control, "Whether the hand can absorb a feel that keeps changing, or needs a few that stay put, is a thing only playing will show." Read beside § Prior art's second warning, the pitch-wheel sentence in § The claim, and the delay line in § Two kinds of force.*

Let me begin with an organist. I took him from Chevalier and have not stopped thinking about him. He arrives at a church whose organ he has never played. It has more manuals or fewer than his own, and the stops are arranged differently. He has an hour before the service. In that hour he does not memorise where the stops are, the way one learns a map. He sits on the bench, works the pedals, pulls the stops, and measures the instrument with his body. He settles into it the way one settles into a house. Then he plays. The stops are no longer places. They are possibilities of expression: a mournful voice down to the left, a bright one under the right hand. What he took in during that hour was not a set of positions. It was a style.

Your page ends its best section on a doubt. I want to answer it, though only your hand can confirm the answer. Bass frets never move, you say. A knob that changes its feel with every parameter it touches is a shifting surface, so perhaps the hand needs a few feels that stay put. My own page gave you that worry. I praised a "stable, predictable" surface as the absorbable kind, and I put it too simply. The organist corrects me. The body schema does not need the world to hold still. The organ changed under him and he took it in within the hour. What the body needs is a physiognomy: wherever the instrument moves, it keeps its way of answering. Habit, I wrote, is knowledge in the hands. It comes only when the body makes the effort, and it cannot be stated apart from that effort. It is the grasp of a meaning, not of a coordinate.

So the question is not one feel or many. It is whether your many feels are a family. Suppose a detent always means "a step in a stepped thing," a stiffening always means "you are leaving the in-tune place," and a hum always means "the patch is speaking now." The hand can absorb those across a hundred parameters, as the organist absorbs a hundred stops, because each feel says the same kind of thing wherever it turns up. What the hand cannot absorb is a feel whose meaning drifts: friction that means distance on one parameter and loudness on the next.

Now your second warning, the string players who did worse with a simulated bow that was "close enough to confuse… but not close enough to promote transfer of skill." This is the same law seen from the other side. Those players already had a bow in their body schema. The near-bow called the old habit up into the arm and then refused it at every stroke. The body reached through the instrument and met the instrument instead, the way a blind man's cane becomes a stick in the hand again. An honest new feel asks for a new habit, and the body is generous with new habits. A counterfeit of an old feel asks the body to unlearn while it plays. You want controllers that "feel like vintage instruments," so this is the edge to walk. Put the vintage in the style: the weight, the slowness, the warmth of the resistance. Do not put it in a forgery of the guitar string, unless the forgery is exact.

Then the pitch wheel, whose spring "pushes back the same way whatever note the hand is reaching for." Gelb and Goldstein had a patient, Schneider, wounded in the war. When a mosquito stung him he found the spot at once, yet he could not point to his nose when asked. Grasping was still alive in him. Pointing, moving toward a target he had to represent, was gone. The pitch wheel turns every player into Schneider. The note becomes an abstract target, out there, reached by pointing and corrected by ear. A string makes the note something to grasp: a place the hand leans into, already inside the movement. Your "pitch you can lean on" gives back concrete movement where the wheel left only the abstract kind.

Last, the motor that senses and pushes. Put your right hand on your left while the left is touching a table. The left hand touches and is touched. The right feels it as a thing, and then, if your attention turns, as a hand that is itself feeling. The two never quite coincide. The reversal is always about to happen and never happens in fact. Your knob is that fold made of copper and magnets, and your page has found its gap without naming it: "delay makes a stiff spring unstable." There is a gap between the hand pushing and the knob pushing back. Where it grows too wide, the knob stops being flesh of my flesh and becomes again an object that bucks. Latency is not only a control problem. It is the width of the chiasm. Keep it narrow enough and the hand forgets which of them moved first, which is the whole of what you want.
