# Graphic Storytelling — Context

What [[Graphic Storytelling]] left out when it was reshaped, and why it is shaped the way it is. The page holds the theory: each tool of sequential art is an expectation the maker sets and then bends. This file holds the craft rules that theory came out of: Toth's staging, the letterer's rules, Chion on sound, the color notes, the lineages and what each one gives a maker. It also holds the sources, and how far each claim was checked.

## Why the page was reshaped

Until 2026-10-29 the page was a borrowed canon of craft rules, and its live edge was one bet: black-and-white plus one bold color, with restraint as the source of the punch. At the Daily Page of 2026-10-28 (sat 2026-10-29), two blind-take strips put that bet to a viewer.

- **The color hold** (2026-10-28). Five takes of one six-panel ink strip on an 84 bpm clock. One red, withheld until the close-up and revealed on its downbeat, scored 5 of 5. All ink scored 4, and red arriving two beats late scored 4. Red shown from the moment the glove falls (panel 3 of 6) and held scored 3, below no color at all. The full palette scored 2. Restraint was necessary but not sufficient. What hit hardest was the color withheld and then revealed on the beat. The timing did as much work as the color.
- **One strip, six stories** (2026-10-29). The same six frames, drawing and clock, with color as the only variable. The two arcs, where the palette moves across the panels, carried far more than the four held moods. The warming take failed because the drawing contradicted it.

Both are one viewer in one sitting. They are readings, not laws.

Loudon took the result further than the page had. Color is one tool among several, and how it ranks against the others is untested. He named the page's real subject: sequential art as a set of tools, each one an expectation the maker bends, in a band between boring and lost. He also added the direction from the page to the screen. Keep the frozen frame and the gap, and add paced reveals, transition types, a soundtrack, and motion inside a held frame. The test for each addition is whether the gap still does its work.

The craft rules below didn't fit that shape at full length. The page keeps one line from most of them, and their full text is here.

## Staging: Eisner's page and Toth's rules in full

- **The page, not the panel** *(Will Eisner)*. Eisner named *sequential art* and treated the page as the unit of composition. His key move for BLUELINE: the balloon is sound made visible, its shape, edge and tail being how a silent medium prints volume, tone and who is speaking. The old page said he coined *graphic novel* too. He popularized it with *A Contract with God* (1978); the fan historian Richard Kyle had used it in 1964 ([Wikipedia, Graphic novel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphic_novel); checked on the web 2026-11-03, not in the palace's sources).
- **Subtract, then commit** *(Alex Toth)*. "Strip it all down to essentials and draw the hell out of what is left." A panel holds a few elements, fully rendered, never many half-rendered ones. That is [[BLUELINE]]'s ink economy and its one figure per panel, stated as a law.
- **Spot your blacks selectively, not evenly** *(Toth)*. Set a deliberate light source for dramatic blacks and drop shadows where the beat is dramatic, and take that shadow work out of the quieter shots. Noir isn't uniform darkness. It is darkness spent on the right panel. Don't render every board at one ink density: save the heavy chiaroscuro for the peaks and let the rest breathe.
- **Shot scale is the pacing engine** *(Toth)*. Move deliberately through wide, one-shot, two-shot, group, close-up and tight close-up, and save the close-up for a face's mood or a small crucial object. On a beat-locked clock, bind shot scale to the song's structure and spend the tight close-up on the emotional peak.
- **Negative space is a positive element.** Bare paper is not emptiness waiting to be filled. It is the shape the ink flows around. In single-figure staging, the void does the isolating.

## The letterer's rules in full

Lettering is not a layer dropped on the art. It is drawn with it.

**Where this overlaps.** [[Typography as Meaning]] (lines 38–56) already holds balloon shape as emotion and channel, the tail as source and addressee, typography as source and manner (italic for the electronic, a distinct face per speaker), Klein's first-speaker-on-the-left, and Blambot's tail stopping about halfway. It also has a conversation's layout rules, which this file lacks. Three rules below are not there: the four caption registers, reserving the balloon's space at board time, and the balloon break. The rendering side belongs to [[Shop/Lettering]]. The rules are kept here in full anyway, by Loudon's decision of 2026-11-03 (below).

- **Lettering is composition** *(Toth)*. "Copy … is part of overall design — it CONTROLS your readers' eye-flow." A balloon's shape, size and position are drawing decisions. That backs BLUELINE's lettering-over-art idea.
- **Reserve the balloon's negative space at board time** *(Todd Klein)*. Modern art often leaves no room for words, because the lettering is no longer planned on the pencils. BLUELINE can do better, because its staging is authored. Carve each balloon's empty space into the board record before the render, so the figure never collides with its own words. And never let a balloon cover a face or the focal point. Put the words in the dead space the composition already left.
- **Balloon order is horizontal placement** *(Klein)*. English reads left to right and top to bottom, so the first speaker's balloon goes on the left. Putting it on the right breaks the reading order. Horizontal position is a timing instrument.
- **Tail geometry** *(Nate Piekos / Blambot)*. The tail points at the mouth as if an invisible line ran on past it to the face, and it ends about 50–60% of the way from balloon to head. BLUELINE already anchors the tail to the OpenPose mouth keypoint; this is the termination rule to encode.
- **Balloon shape is the vocal channel** *(Blambot)*. The container carries meaning, not only the words. A whisper is a dashed outline, a shout a spiky burst, physical distress a wavy edge. A radio, phone, TV or any speaker is italic text in a rectangular "radio" balloon. A thought is a tail of small bubbles. The standard rule: italic marks transmitted or electronic speech.
- **Four caption registers** *(Blambot)*. Captions are not one voice. Keep apart (a) location and time stamps, (b) internal monologue, (c) an off-camera spoken voice, and (d) the editorial or author's voice. Each is its own channel with its own box. They map almost one to one onto BLUELINE's authored text voices, so give each register its own face and box.
- **Typography as emotion.** Bold weight signals emphasis and volume. A distinct typeface per character makes voices legible without a tail. Hand tremor reads as instability. (Sourced; it matches BLUELINE's per-voice type plan.)
- **The balloon break.** Isolate the last word of a line in its own small balloon to force a pause and land the emphasis. It is a timing device drawn in ink, and it fits BLUELINE's clock exactly: a broken-out word can hit on the beat while the rest of the line sits before it.

## Time: closure, compression and the reveal

- **Closure is the motion budget** *(Scott McCloud)*. Comics are sequential art, and the real action happens in the **gutter**, the blank gap between panels. The reader supplies the missing motion and time there. McCloud calls this closure, and it is why a beat-locked sequence of stills reads as continuous motion at all: the viewer completes it. For a beat-locked film it is the licence behind held poses and limited animation. You don't have to draw the in-between if the cut and the beat make the viewer supply it. He maps the panel-to-panel transitions: moment, action, subject, scene, aspect, non-sequitur.
- **A note on "time is space."** The old page said McCloud shows that time is rendered as space, so that "a wider panel, or more gutter before the next, simply lasts longer." The phrase is his. The "simply" and the gutter half were the old page's own strengthening, put in his mouth. Secondary sources give his hedges: a panel's durations are "defined more by the contents of the panel than by the panel itself," and differences of shape "can affect the reading experience." No source was found for a wider gutter lasting longer. This was checked against secondary sources only, not the book (about pp. 99–101). The page now says it that way, and the rich face's time-is-space piece times the panel half and the gutter half apart. No reader has run it yet.
- **Compression and decompression.** A whole war in one panel, or one gesture stretched across six. Compression jolts; decompression dilates and dwells. On the clock, decompression is holding a board across more beats, and compression is a hard cut on the downbeat.
- **The reveal and the turn.** Comics hide the next panel until the eye arrives, and the page-turn is a trap built for surprise. BLUELINE's version is the beat: the thing withheld until the downbeat lands is the reveal.

## Sound: Chion

Michel Chion writes about film, but the ideas carry over.

- **Diegetic and non-diegetic.** Sound is inside the story world (diegetic) or outside it (non-diegetic: narration, score).
- **The acousmêtre.** A voice with no visible body holds an uncanny power.
- **Added value** (*la valeur ajoutée*). Sound and image together make a whole larger than the sum, so the song is changing what the frame means.
- **Sound structures the image's time.** It animates a still, puts disordered action in a line, and *vectorizes* a scene toward a goal. For a beat-locked film those two ideas matter more than the diegetic split. BLUELINE's song is not an accompaniment: it is the frame's clock and half its meaning.
- **Sound vectorizes the sequence.** Because sound drives the eye toward a goal and gives disordered images a readable order, the song can carry a sequence the stills alone would leave unclear. Lean on it: let the music put the frames in order, so the frames can be sparse.

## Color: the old live edge

The old page's edge was BLUELINE's locked look, stark black ink on rough paper, broken a little: black-and-white with one deliberate color, for impact. The specifics:

- **Spot color, the color hold.** Almost everything stays black and white. One element is colored, so the eye goes straight to it and the meaning rides along. The *Sin City* red is the example: a dress, blood, brake lights, the thing that matters being the only thing in color.
- **Restraint as the source of the punch.** One accent against ink hits harder than a full palette, because the page has taught the reader that color is rare and so means something. The second and third colors spend that capital fast. The color hold finished this rule rather than breaking it. Restraint was necessary but not sufficient: red held from the glove's fall scored below no color, and the take that scored 5 was the one where the strip taught its absence and then revealed it on the beat.
- **The color script.** Plan the one or two color choices across the whole piece in advance as an emotional through-line, the way film and animation map light before a frame is made. For BLUELINE this ties cleanly to the beat: the accent can arrive on a musical event. One strip, six stories is the first test of it. The arcs, color moving across time, carried more than any held palette.
- **Flat color, not rendered color** *(Dave Stewart on Hellboy)*. Stewart gets depth and graphic power from broad flat color laid into Mignola's simplified black shapes, the opposite of the industry's complicated 3-D rendering. He re-keys the whole scheme per scene as a "visual cue of a change," laying base flats first and using value and hue to lead the eye. That is a color script in miniature. For BLUELINE's pen-flow world it is the model: a flat field of one hue behind the ink, re-keyed per section.
- **The two-ink look** *(risograph, duotone)*. For the screenprint look, the production logic is simple: each ink becomes its own greyscale stencil (a channel separation), printed in two passes, black plus one spot ink. It is the analog ancestor of black-and-white plus one color.

The questions this thread was meant to answer: which color, how often, attached to what, when silence (pure black-and-white) is the louder choice, and, on a beat-locked clock, where in the bar the accent lands.

## Lineages, and what to steal from each

The traditions closest to BLUELINE's ink-noir bias, each with one thing to steal:

- **Frank Miller, *Sin City*.** Pure black shapes are the chiaroscuro; form is implied by where the white isn't. Its selective color does two jobs at once: focus (the eye snaps to the one colored thing) and symbol (the color means danger, desire, death). Steal: let black shapes carry the drawing, and make any color do double duty.
- **Mike Mignola and Dave Stewart, *Hellboy*.** Negative-space ink noir: huge flat blacks, figures reduced to silhouette, depth built from flat color fields rather than rendering. Steal: shadow as a designed shape, not an absence of light.
- **Moebius and the *bande dessinée*, the *ligne claire*.** The clear, even line, flat color, vast calm surreal worlds drawn with total confidence. Steal: the clean contour holding a strange world without hysteria, so the surreal reads as matter-of-fact.
- **The woodcut wordless novel, Frans Masereel and Lynd Ward.** Whole stories told in relief-print sequences with no words at all, each page composed to stand alone and hand the story on. It is the closest historical cousin to BLUELINE's model, wordless art with text lettered over as a separate layer, and proof the pictures can carry the whole load before a word arrives. Steal: make each board legible as pure image first; the words are a second channel, not a crutch.
- **German Expressionist ink.** Distortion, jagged shadow, psychological space over accurate space. Steal: bend the staging to the feeling when the beat is extreme.
