Does a wider panel last longer?

The page’s line, after McCloud: time is drawn as space, and a wider panel or a wider gutter lasts longer. This walks up to that claim from the gap between two panels, and ends on a test your hands take, blind. Nothing here makes a sound.

two panels · one gap
what the gap held · what you drew without being shown
drag an edge · the amber marks are handles
the stopwatch · nothing to read yet

Hold for about one second.

what your hands said · every hold, by layout · hollow rings are the same story, plain
r = h(layout, story) ⁄ h(plain, story)
(how long you held for one story in one layout) ÷ (how long you held for the same story, plain). Above 1, that layout lasted longer for you. Dividing by the same story's plain hold takes out how long each story's wait is to begin with.
P(all three r ≥ 1.1 | the layout does nothing) ≤ (½)³ = 1 ⁄ 8
"Longer" here means longer by a tenth or more in all three stories (shorter: by the same, the other way). If the layout made no difference, each story would land there at most half the time, so all three would by chance at most one run in eight. So "longer in all three" is a reading worth noticing, not a proof. The chance that either half, panel or gutter, shows "longer" by chance is at most 1 in 4; the two are called split only when the wide panel's hold beats the wide gutter's by a tenth in all three stories, or loses by as much (again at most 1 in 8).
Three small stories (a door, a kettle, a bus), each drawn once; only the layout changes. The wide panel and the wide gutter each add the same length along the reading line, 102 px at full size; the tall panel adds the same area as the wide one, but across the line, so it asks whether your hands answer size or the line. Each strip is drawn at a slightly different scale (0.9 to 1.1) so absolute size gives nothing away. Checked offline on this piece's own engine (the script marked engine): the five layouts' geometry, the shuffle (every story in every layout once, no story or layout twice running), and the verdict on made-up hands. Blind only in part: you see every layout, and the page above states the claim. What stays unsaid is which comparisons count, and the answer is a hold, not a choice. The tall control tells size from the line; nothing here can rule out that you held longer because you know what the page says. No reader has taken the test yet: what it shows is yours.
