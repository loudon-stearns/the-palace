# ACT reading — sources

Read from the web on 2026-11-10 by a maker who has read no ACT book whole. Each line says where it came from. "Summary" means I read a page's summary through a fetch tool, not the page itself.

## What ACT is

- Acceptance and Commitment Therapy: Steven C. Hayes, Kirk Strosahl, Kelly Wilson, *Acceptance and Commitment Therapy* (Guilford, 1999; second edition 2011/2012). Not read; cited everywhere below.
- **Six processes** of psychological flexibility (the "hexaflex"): acceptance, cognitive defusion, being present, self as context, values, committed action. The first four are called the mindfulness and acceptance processes, the last two the behaviour-change ones. Acceptance is "the active and aware embrace of those private events occasioned by one's history"; defusion aims to change how a person relates to a thought, not its form or how often it comes. Source: ACBS, "The Six Core Processes of ACT", https://contextualscience.org/the_six_core_processes_of_act (summary).
- **Experiential avoidance**: the effort to escape or avoid private experiences (feelings, memories, thoughts), proposed as a functional dimension under many troubles. Hayes, Wilson, Gifford, Follette and Strosahl, 1996; Hayes and Gifford, 1997. Source: ACBS publication pages and the University of Nevada archive, found by search (summary).
- **Control is the problem / creative hopelessness; clean and dirty discomfort.** ACBS's study summary of the 1999 book, pages 136–141: clean discomfort comes and goes with living and can't be controlled; dirty discomfort is "discomfort over discomfort", made by the effort to control; it fades when willingness is high and control low. https://contextualscience.org/act_book_summary_pages_136_141 (summary). I looked for the 1999 book's own sentence "if you aren't willing to have it, you've got it" and could not confirm it from a source, so the piece does not use it.

## The exercises, by name

From ACBS, "Techniques and Components" (posts by Elizabeth Gifford, Steven Hayes and Kirk Strosahl, 2005 and 2008; the page says it is not officially approved), https://contextualscience.org/book/export/html/29 (summary):

- Under creative hopelessness / control is a problem: the person in the hole, Chinese handcuffs, **tug-of-war with a monster** (the goal is to drop the rope, not win), the feedback screech, the polygraph (consequences of control), **the two scales** (willingness against control), the box full of stuff.
- Under defusion: "I'm having the thought that…", buying thoughts, Titchener's repetition, physicalizing, monsters (passengers) on the bus, take your mind for a walk.
- Under acceptance: **the Serenity Prayer**, the tin-can monster, clean and dirty emotions, willingness is not wanting, the bum at the door, the willingness question.
- Under self as context: the observer exercise, the chessboard.
- **Soldiers in the parade** and **leaves on a stream**: ACBS's summary of the book's pages 158–168 (chapter 6, "Building Acceptance by Defusing Language"): thoughts go by on placards, or on leaves, and the parade stopping is the sign of having been hooked. https://contextualscience.org/act_book_summary_pages_158_168 (summary). Many handout sites credit leaves on a stream to Russ Harris, *ACT Made Simple* (2009); I did not see either book's script.
- **The struggle switch**, Russ Harris, *The Happiness Trap*: a switch at the back of the mind; on, every discomfort is a thing to fight. Secondary handouts only (University of Iowa wellness tip; Mentalyc, citing Harris 2006, "Embracing your demons").

## The one the piece adapts

Russ Harris, **"Pushing away paper"**, 2017, © Russ Harris, www.ImLearningACT.com, read whole as a PDF: https://www.actmindfully.com.au/upimages/Pushing_Away_Paper_-_2017_version.pdf

What it does, in my words: two people sit side by side facing the room; out in the room is everything that matters, the problems to deal with and the day's tasks; a sheet of paper stands for the thoughts and feelings one wants rid of; hold it tight and push it as far away as the arms go; notice three things (how tiring, how distracting, how hard to do anything else); drop it into the lap; notice the difference; notice it has not gone, and that there is a new way of responding to it. Harris says it evolved from "pushing the clipboard" in *ACT Made Simple*, that it is "only a metaphor; it won't give clients the ability to accept", and warns against doing it with anyone who has a painful neck, spine or shoulders.

What the piece keeps: the room, the pushing, the three notices in his order, the drop to the lap, "it hasn't gone". What it changes: a held press for stretched arms (far smaller; the tap setting is the answer to his warning); a stone for the paper; no therapist beside you; his humour ("dislocate those shoulders") left out; his closing point that a painful feeling may carry useful information left out.

## Stoicism and ACT

- Epictetus, fragment 9, in Aulus Gellius, *Attic Nights* 19.1: a Stoic turns pale in a storm and explains from Epictetus that impressions force themselves on us, while assent is voluntary; the sage is moved for a moment and does not agree. https://fgh.perseids.org/read/latinLit/phi1254/phi001/perseus-eng1/19.1 (search summary; the page itself returned an error when fetched).
- Donald Robertson, "“Distancing” versus “Disputation” as the central process of Stoic psychotherapy", https://donaldrobertson.name/?p=5346 (summary): the Stoics leaned more on stepping back from an impression (*Enchiridion* 1: "You are merely an impression and not at all what you appear to be") than on arguing with it; Beck called this distancing; ACT was first called comprehensive distancing and holds that disputing can get in the way of it. Robertson advocates for Stoicism; this is one reading.
- Albert Ellis credited Epictetus for the idea under REBT. Search summary only.
