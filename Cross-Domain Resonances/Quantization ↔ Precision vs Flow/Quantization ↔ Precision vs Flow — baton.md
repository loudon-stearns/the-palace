**The 2026-11-08 reframe, Loudon's:** we hear change in timing better than absolute distance. The parallel is relative versus absolute pitch: most ears track the interval, few hold the note. A quantize grid measures distance from a line, which is what we hear worst.

**The three leads from the sit, checked 2026-11-10. Each survives only narrowed.**
- *Negative mean asynchrony* is a finger-tapping finding. Tapping to a steady click, people land a few tens of milliseconds early (Aschersleben 2002, abstract read). It does not show that drummers in a band play ahead: in jazz ensembles soloists sit behind the drummer on the downbeat (Friberg & Sundström 2002, read on KTH's summary, not the paper).
- *The fielder cancelling optical acceleration* is one model of the catch, and a contested one. McBeath et al. 1995 proposed a rival and noted that people judge acceleration poorly; Fink, Foo & Warren 2009 found for cancellation in virtual reality (abstracts read). It is a picture of steering by nulling a change. It is not evidence about hearing.
- *A timing JND of 10 to 20 ms* depends on tempo: about 10 ms when notes are under 240 ms apart, about 5% of the gap when slower, and higher inside real music (Friberg & Sundberg 1993, ASA abstract opened; Repp 2002, abstract read).

5. **Loudon's next test, now four questions.** Every experiment so far treats human "error" as random and symmetric around the beat. From listening to and playing with drummers, his intuition is that it isn't. Is a drummer's error lopsided, leaning generally behind the beat (dragging) or ahead of it (rushing)? Is feel the shifting of that spread? He said: *"and perhaps 'feel' is the shifting of this distribution."* Carry that as his reading. And, from the 2026-11-08 reframe: do listeners hear timing change more easily than fixed error, and does that come with training or with being human? Study first: research the microtiming literature, and treat the three leads above as leads. Then design a test. Do a player's onset distributions shift with musical context (into a chorus, after a fill)? Do listeners hear the shift as feel, and hear a change more readily than a constant offset of the same size? One way in: analyse real drum takes' onset deviation distributions. Another: a rough whose "mistakes" are skewed rather than gaussian, with a distribution that moves through the phrase, compared by ear. Bring the design to Loudon before running anything at scale.

   **Read for the study, 2026-11-10** (abstracts unless marked):
   - The body corrects timing changes the ear misses: Repp 2000, *Psychological Research* 63(2).
   - Sensitivity to a tempo change grows with the number of intervals heard: Drake & Botte 1993, *Perception & Psychophysics* 54(3).
   - Where a beat is heard depends on the sound's attack and length: Danielsen et al. 2019, *JEP:HPP* 45(3). This backs the timbre calibration below.
   - Real deviations are not random noise, and listeners prefer them to white-noise humanizing: Hennig et al. 2011, *PLoS ONE* 6(10) (opened). One track, Porcaro's hi-hat on "I Keep Forgettin'", shows slow drifts and two-bar patterns: Räsänen et al. 2015, *PLoS ONE* 10(6). This is the nearest support for "error isn't symmetric random".
   - Told to lay back, most drummers also played louder: Danielsen et al. 2015, *JASA* 138(4).
   - Contested, whether microtiming makes groove at all. Keil says yes (1987, 1995; details from search only). Listening studies mostly say no: Senn et al. 2016, *Frontiers in Psychology* 7, found quantized and original rated equally. Partial support for Keil: Kilchenmann & Senn 2015, *Frontiers in Psychology* 6.
   - Listeners implicitly track the mean and the variance of microtiming: Kaplan, Jamone & Pearce 2023, *Cognition* 239.

   **Apparently untested; nothing was found:** a constant offset against a moving one of the same size in real grooves; whether a player's onset spread skews and shifts with the section; whether listeners hear a shifting skewed spread as intent.

   **A caution for the design:** with no reference, a constant offset from a grid cannot be heard at all. Distance is only audible as asynchrony against another voice. So the fair comparison is a constant asynchrony against a changing one. The thresholds that exist give no clear edge to change.

## Calibrations from this session
- Loudon's correction to the first rough: feel speaks on many levels at once, and each measure sets up the next. Snapping to the bar groove keeps what repeats and flattens the phrase. Rough two was built on that.
- The rough held timbre fixed to isolate time. That separation is itself the error, a mistake in prosody. Mechanical sounds suit mechanical time, so "too stiff" is not wrong for them. Do not build the next piece with one drum kit.
- The page's "50% sounds more musical" partly held: 50%, 75% and the take's own groove all grooved. Snapping to the take's own groove tied; it did not win. Do not write it up as the answer.
- The rough's model of the gesture can be wrong at its most important moment, and only the ear catches it: rough 3's no-click push gave up at the turnaround until Loudon heard it. Do not report a rough as working before someone plays it.
- A clip should be as short as the thing it tests. If a bar is scheduled ahead, Stop must cut a bus.
- Write HTML with Bash heredocs or `cp`, never the Write tool: Write opens the file in the browser pane ([[The Daily Page — tuning]] items 19 and 27).
