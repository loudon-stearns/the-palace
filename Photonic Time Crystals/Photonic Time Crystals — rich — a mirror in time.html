Photonic time crystals · open question one

A mirror in time

The page asks what time-reversed audio from a time-modulated system sounds like, and whether it is anything more than a reverse buffer. One phrase goes into a long uniform line and comes back off a wall, off a jump of the whole line’s index, and off a jump and a jump back. Then the two time mirrors meet the strongest digital rivals a reverse buffer has. Five steps, the last one the test.

1 · A mirror in space

A phrase of four notes goes into a long line from where you stand. At the far end a wall sends it back. The first note to reach the wall is the first to come home, so the echo keeps the phrase’s order. That is the ordinary mirror: it reverses direction in space, never order in time.

2 · A mirror in time

No wall now; the line runs on. Once the whole phrase is inside it, the whole line’s index jumps at one instant, from n₁ to n₂, everywhere at once. Nothing in space has changed, so every wave keeps its wave number; only its speed and frequency can change. Part of every wave turns round. The note that went in last is nearest, so it comes home first: the phrase returns reversed. And since the line is now slower, it comes back lower and longer.

at an instant, nothing can jump in the fields that carry the stored charge and flux; so the wave number stays, the frequency scales by the ratio of the indices, and the backward wave’s electric field is this fraction of the incoming one

3 · There and back

Jump the index up and, a moment τ later, back down. Now the returning copy travels at the old speed, so its pitch is the phrase’s own. But it is two turned copies, one from each jump, a hair apart in time and of opposite sign: a comb, a temporal Fabry–Pérot. Some frequencies come back strongly, some not at all, and the lowest hardly come back.

|r(f)| = ½ · |n₂/n₁ − n₁/n₂| · |sin(2π f τ · n₁/n₂)|
what returns is half the difference of the index ratio and its inverse, times a sine of how many half-cycles fit in the time the line was held, at the frequency the wave had while it was held

4 · Two digital rivals

A plain reverse buffer was never the strong rival. Step 2 already sounded like tape played backward slowly, and step 3 like a reverse through a comb. So build both, in plain DSP, from the rule’s own numbers. For the single jump: reverse the phrase, read it n₂/n₁ times slower, scale it by r_E. For the there-and-back: reverse the phrase, add it to itself delayed 2τ·n₁/n₂ with the opposite sign, both scaled by ¼(n₂/n₁ − n₁/n₂). Listen to each beside the mirror it imitates.

jump: y(t) = r_E · R(t·n₁/n₂) there and back: y(t) = K · [R(t − d/2) − R(t + d/2)] , K = ¼(n₂/n₁ − n₁/n₂) , d = 2τ·n₁/n₂
R is the phrase reversed; one rival reads it slower and quieter, the other adds it to a copy of itself a hair later and turned over

5 · Which reverse?

The test. First the rule, before any number: a time mirror counts as a sound of its own only if no chain of reverse, varispeed and comb comes within −40 dB of it, measured as the energy of the difference against the mirror’s own. If a chain does, then in this ideal line the mirror is that chain, and the finding is which operation it adds to a reverse. Then the numbers: the strongest rivals and a plain reverse against each mirror, what each does to order, pitch, length and loudness, and the mixing rule checked against Maxwell’s equations on a Yee grid that knows nothing of it.

The line here is lossless and has no dispersion, and the jumps are instant. Whatever could give a physical time mirror a sound of its own lies outside that ideal: a switch with a finite rise (Moussa et al.’s took about 3 ns), a material whose speed depends on frequency, and, in a periodic time crystal, gain in the momentum gap (the piece above, step 3). The level is the electric field, read where the phrase went in. The page also says time-reflection happens “at bandgap frequencies”: the single jump turns every frequency round at once; only a periodic run of jumps, the crystal, makes it strong and selective.
