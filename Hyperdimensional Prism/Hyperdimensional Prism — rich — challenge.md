## The blind test: chance, arms, leaks

- **Chance and rule.** Stated before the first set: 5 of 10, pass at 9 or more. A coin guesser passes with p = 11/1024, one in 93, as stated. A guesser who uses the stated 5/5 split can only reach 9 by reaching 10: one in 252. Both conservative.
- **Both arms.** Scored apart for the eye and for the count. Answering "one" every time (the page's lean) scored 5/10, one 5/5, four 0/5 in my browser run, as the piece says it will.
- **Leaks.** During the ten sets the readout shows only "answered k of 10"; the score table and the count appear after the tenth answer. Opening step 5 mid-test shows the fixed demo set, not the current one. Order is a seeded shuffle; all ten sets are built before the first is shown, so no timing cue. A single shadow's distribution is the same for both kinds by construction (one object then four lights, or four objects each with one light, all from the same draws), so no single-shadow tell exists. I looked for a joint tell that skips the links: the spread of curve length across a set's four shadows separates the kinds at best 55.6% (2000 sets each), which would pass the 9/10 rule 2.5% of the time against 1.1% by chance. Radius kurtosis: 51.5%. Not a usable leak. It does mean the shared object leaves a faint trace outside the links, which matters for one sentence below.

## The pieces

The count's one-object arm cannot fail: four linear shadows of a four-dimensional curve have rank at most 4 by algebra. The four-object arm and the eye can fail. The piece also never says the shadows must be straight: I bent each one-object shadow through tanh and the count read four in 200 of 200, so a real one object reads as four under a curved projection.
3. **Walks up.** Yes: a cube's shadow, a flat square that casts the same one, a four-dimensional curve, four shadows linked by one moment, the count, then the test.

## The offers

1. **The Visual Identity, phase not ratio — disputed.** The counts are right: row A at most 3 (an identity), row B 7 at random phases (5 at 0 and π), confirmed by numpy SVD. The refutation is right about the math and overreaches about the page (tuning 12a): it holds only once the hidden object is held to four dimensions, and the page says "higher-dimensional." Under the maker's own rule in offers 3 and 4, row B (7 < 8) is one object, a seven-dimensional one; all four figures are lights on the curve (sin kt, cos kt), k = 1 to 5. The phase half of the proposed sentence is an identity worth keeping as a gloss ("the phase turns the light on one fixed curve"); the ratio half ("changes the curve itself") is true only under the cap the page does not set. Not a claim-change as written; at most an addition of the phase half, held for Loudon.
2. **Open Questions, third question — disputed as to kind; math confirmed.** sin(bt + φ) = cos φ · sin bt + sin φ · cos bt; the engine agrees to 1.78e-15. That is a trig identity, so this restates a definition: a no-claim-edit at most (tuning 12e). It also answers an easier question than the page asks: any flat curve (x, y) is trivially a shadow of (x, y, x², y²), error 0, so "it already does" holds for every figure. The real question, showing a higher-dimensional object being projected, is still open. The phase-turns-the-light reading belongs in the Context, or as a no-claim gloss.
3. **Open Questions, first question — confirmed, with an amendment.** Links lost: one object reads four, 1000 of 1000. Object allowed 8 dimensions: everything reads one, 1000 of 1000, and that cannot fail, since eight rows never need more than eight directions. Below 8 the count still tells them apart: cap 7 and cap 5, 500 of 500. The amendment: the projection must be straight. A one-object set with each shadow bent through tanh reads four, 200 of 200. And with links kept but bending allowed, every linked set is one thing, the shared clock, a one-dimensional curve. So the proposed sentence needs "a straight (linear) projection" beside "which point is which" and "smaller than its four views together". The offer is for the page's own Open Questions, as it says.
4. **Cross-Pillar Connections, a test for "not analogies" — disputed.** The rule is only shown on synthetic curves, and the page's examples do not have the thing it needs. Kuramoto in synthesis, conversation, ethics and mycorrhizae share a law, not a set of points matched one to one across domains, so the rank test has nothing to stack. Its last clause ("a match you chose is an analogy") would make every cross-domain match an analogy, which is the opposite of the page's sentence, not a test of it. And by this rule row B in offer 1 is one object, so offers 1 and 4 cannot both stand as written. Hold it as an open question (what would the matches between pillars be?), not as text.

## The face as a whole

It makes the page's idea felt more than most faces do. "One thing, many shadows" is easy to nod at, and the opening piece turns it into something you can lose: shuffle the links and one object becomes four; let the hidden thing grow and every four things become one. That is a real finding about what the page's metaphor would need to mean something. The scope piece is lovely to walk and the torus curve behind the scope is a good find. Where the face goes wrong is the step from "on these curves" to "the page is wrong": both verdicts promote a model's number into a judgment of the text, and the scope verdict uses a four-dimension cap that the opening piece itself shows is the whole question. Fix those sentences and the face is a test, not busy.
