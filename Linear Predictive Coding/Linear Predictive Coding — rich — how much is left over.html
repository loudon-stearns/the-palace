How much is left over

The page's one equation says each sample is a weighted sum of the samples before it, plus a leftover, the residual. Three steps: a sine, which two weights predict exactly; noise, which no number of weights predicts at all; and then a voice, which sits between the two. The last step is the test: the better the prediction, the less there is to send.

1 · Predict a sine from its last sample

Guess the next sample of a sine from the one before it. One weight gets close, and closer the lower the note. Two weights get it exactly: from then on the sine carries no news.

The weights are the best ones for a sine that runs forever, from Levinson-Durbin on its exact autocorrelation, cos(ωk). The leftover plays at its true level beside the sine, so silence is silence.

2 · What no weights can predict

Wold (1938): a steady random signal splits into a part its own past predicts and a part it doesn't, the innovation. Add weights and the predictable part goes. The innovation stays, at any order.

One second at 16 kHz; weights from the signal's own autocorrelation over that second. Two sines at 300 and 1700 Hz; white noise; a 440 Hz sine with white noise 17 dB under it. The leftover plays at its true level.

3 · A voice, weight by weight

Now a voice, saying the page's own sentence. A fresh set of weights every 10 ms, read from the 25 ms of voice around it. Raise the order: the leftover shrinks, the voice's resonances move out of it and into the weights, and what's left is the part you'd still have to send.

The 6.02 dB per bit is the textbook rule for coding a waveform finely at a fixed error; it counts what the leftover saves, not what the weights cost to send. The weights here are not quantized. The leftover is played as loud as the voice unless you ask for its true level. The voice is Kokoro, an open-weights speech model, saying the page's sentence; the piece reads it from a copy it carries.
