One step at a time

An audio engine never solves the equation. It takes a step: reads the rule where the sound is now, and moves a little. The size of the step, and the way it is taken, leave an error in the sound. Silent until you press a button.

One step

The fade from the first piece, τ · dy/dt + y = u, taken in steps. At each step the engine reads the slope where the level stands, walks one step along it, and reads again. The straight walk misses the curve a little each time. Make the step bigger and hear it: the level moves in stairs and arrives early, ahead of the curve, and past one step per τ it overshoots.

ynext = y + h · (u − y) / τ
(the level after one step) = (the level now) + (the step, h) × (the slope the rule gives here). This is Euler's step. The true curve would have kept e−h/τ of the gap; this keeps 1 − h/τ.

A 48 kHz engine on a 10 ms fade takes steps of 0.002 τ: set the slider all the way left and the miss is of the way. For a fade, the step hardly matters. Past 1 τ the steps overshoot, and past 2 τ they grow without end.

Step a swing

Now the same walk on a swing: the page's second-order equation, a 220 Hz ring that should fade over two seconds. Euler's step goes straight along the circle's edge, so it always lands a little outside it. Every step the swing grows, and only friction can pay it back. Count the steps per cycle down and hear how soon it runs away.

size after one step = √(1 + (ωh)²) × size before
(how big the swing is after a step) = (the square root of one, plus the angle the step should turn, squared) × (how big it was). It is always more than one, so friction has to pay it back every step or the ring grows.

Other ways to take the step

Euler isn't the only step. Read the slope at the end of the step instead of the start, and the swing shrinks instead of grows. Update the speed first and use the new speed for the position, and the size holds but the pitch drifts. Average the two ends, and the size holds and the pitch drifts the other way. Runge–Kutta reads the slope four times a step. And for a linear equation there is an exact step, which is the difference equation a modal resonator runs. Each rule's error has a sign and a sound.

(y, v)next = M · (y, v)
(position and speed after one step) = (a two-by-two table of numbers each rule fixes) × (position and speed now). The table turns the swing by some angle each step, which is its pitch, and scales it by some amount, which is its fade. A rule's whole error is how far those two are from the true ones.

At 48 kHz, does it matter?

The page asks how much the difference matters for audio. A real engine steps once a sample, so what counts is how many samples each cycle gets. A struck bar has five partials, each its own copy of the page's equation, and the top ones get few samples: at 48 kHz a 440 Hz bar's fifth partial, near 5.9 kHz, gets eight. Pick a rule; each partial is stepped at the real sample rate, then its pitch and ring are read off the sound.

The rule, set before the run: a step rule passes on a partial if its pitch is within 5 cents of the exact one and its ring within 10% of the exact ring; it fails if it misses either, or runs away. The difference matters at a rate and pitch where some rule fails.
