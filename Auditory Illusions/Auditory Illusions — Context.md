# Auditory Illusions — Context

What the page left out, and why it is shaped this way.

## Why it is shaped as a climb

Loudon asked (2026-11-04, under the working name Sonic Hallucinations) for a page presenting all the sonic illusions we can find, as probes of perception, so that explaining them teaches the physics and the psychoacoustics. A flat list would do the presenting and miss the teaching. Ordered by where in the hearing system each one happens — air, cochlea, the two ears, pitch, grouping, meaning, time, silence — each illusion becomes evidence that its stage exists and makes a particular assumption. The order is the explanation.

The visual twins came second, at Loudon's ask (2026-11-04), with the Shepard tone and the barber pole as his example. Each twin is graded mechanism or shape because the palace's rule is that a forced connection is not a find; the grading keeps the strong pairs (Huggins pitch and the stereogram, continuity and amodal completion, musical ear and Charles Bonnet) from lending false weight to the merely pretty ones. Shepard's question paired continuity with a Kanizsa edge and streaming with Rubin's vase; the page moved both to closer twins and gave Kanizsa to the missing fundamental.

It grew from the [[Shepard Tone Synthesizer]]'s sixth open question, which asked whether the family of auditory illusions wanted its own entry and named it [[Auditory Illusions]] before it existed; the 2026-10-24 Weave ruled that ghost an invitation and left it to Loudon. He chose that name over his working one, Sonic Hallucinations, so the ghost closes as written. The word *hallucination* stayed in the body on purpose: the palace elsewhere uses it almost only for a model's failure (Hofstadter's page and dossier, and Agnes Martin's dossier, are the exceptions), and this page holds that it is also how hearing works.

## Where to hear them

- **ASA / IPO *Auditory Demonstrations*** — Houtsma, Rossing and Wagenaars, for the Acoustical Society of America and the Institute for Perception Research, Eindhoven. 39 sections on how hearing works: masking, critical bands, pitch, the missing fundamental, Shepard tones.
- **Bregman and Ahad, *Demonstrations of Auditory Scene Analysis*** — 41 demonstrations, with a booklet keyed to Bregman's book. Streaming, continuity, grouping.
- **Diana Deutsch** — the CDs *Musical Illusions and Paradoxes* (octave, scale, chromatic, glissando illusions, tritone paradox, mysterious melody) and *Phantom Words and Other Curiosities* (2003, with speech to song), the book *Musical Illusions and Phantom Words*, and audio on her UCSD pages.
- **Yoshitaka Nakajima** — demonstrations of the gap transfer illusion and related time illusions, collected in a 2006 *Acoustical Science and Technology* review.
- The Redwood Center wiki links audio for the first three collections. The **Best Illusion of the Year** contest takes auditory entries, though its winners so far have mostly been visual.

## The research, by thread

- **Vision and hearing grouping alike.** Bregman, *Auditory Scene Analysis* (1990), showed the auditory scene follows the Gestalt laws first found for vision. Aksentijevic, Elliott and Barber (*Visual Cognition*, 2001) found grouping in apparent motion, streaming and static displays behaves alike. Remez (*Psychological Review*, 1994) is the skeptic: Gestalt principles describe and do not explain, and they are a weak base for speech. Bregman reportedly said much the same of his own grouping rules.
- **No review compares auditory and visual illusions head to head** that the 2026-11-04 search found. The twins on the page are assembled pair by pair from each field's own literature. That gap may be worth something.
- **Huggins pitch** — Cramer and Huggins, *JASA* 30 (1958). Culling, Summerfield and Marshall (*JASA*, 1998) explain it as binaural unmasking. Calling it the auditory random-dot stereogram is common; who said it first was not found.
- **Tritone paradox** — Deutsch (1986); Deutsch, North and Ray (1990) on the link to speaking range; Deutsch (*Music Perception* 8, 1991) on California against southern England.
- **Octave illusion** — Deutsch (1974); the what/where two-channel model (Deutsch and Roll, 1976, and after). Chambers, Mattingley and Moss (2002) argue against it. Brancucci and others (MEG, 2018) found activity in both pathways and propose calling it the Deutsch illusion, since it isn't limited to the octave.
- **Sound-induced flash** — Shams, Kamitani and Shimojo, *Nature* (2000); a 2020 review in *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews* covers twenty years and over a hundred studies.
- **Zwicker tone** — Zwicker, "Negative afterimage in hearing," *JASA* (1964). Franosch and others (2003) model it as noise reduction with asymmetric inhibition, not fatigue; prevalence estimates run from about half to over 85% depending on the noise.
- **Spectral motion aftereffect** — Shu and others (1993); spatial auditory motion aftereffect, Grantham and Wightman (1979), weaker than vision's.
- **Rhythm into pitch** — Ungan and Yagcioglu (*Frontiers in Psychology*, 2014): tapping below about 20 clicks a second, pitch above about 50, worst discrimination in 20–33. One small study; the boundary moves with how it is defined.
- **Musical ear syndrome** — clinical case literature calls it auditory Charles Bonnet syndrome; some reviews argue the Bonnet label should stay visual.
- **Shepard tables** — Shepard, *Mind Sights* (1990), "Turning the Tables."

## Left out, for now

- **The wagon wheel** — it belongs to sampling, not to perception; the Shepard project's open question already pairs it with digital aliasing.
- Deutsch's **glissando**, **chromatic** and **mysterious melody** illusions, siblings of the scale illusion.
- **Equal loudness** — the Fletcher–Munson curves, not an illusion so much as the shape of the instrument.
- The **cocktail party effect** and **comodulation masking release** — real and good, but streaming carries their lesson here.
- **Binaural beat entrainment** as a claim, kept to one honest line.

## Bridges drawn

- **Whisper's phantom phrases** ([[Whisper]], § Limits) against Deutsch's phantom words: a brain and a model, each built to find meaning, making it up when the input is thin. Cousins rather than twins: the model's input is absent, Deutsch's is ambiguous. `mirrors` names the shared move.
- Considered, not drawn: Anil Seth's "controlled hallucination" against the [[Registry Pattern]] (generation held in check by a verified vocabulary). It may be real, but the page doesn't argue it yet, and Seth's wording wants checking against *Being You* (2021) before it is quoted.

## Open questions

1. Which illusion is the second build, after the Shepard tone? Huggins pitch needs only noise and headphones; the continuity illusion only a tone and a burst; both are a few lines of code.
2. Should the page grow a rich face — one playable demo per row of the side-by-side table, the sound and its visual twin together?
3. Is there a real find in the missing twins? The page claims the twinless illusions are the time-of-arrival ones; that wants testing against the eye's twinless list.
4. Deutsch's Shepard-tone experiments sit next to the synthesizer's stages. Does the tritone paradox belong on the [[Shepard Tone Synthesizer]] as its next stage — a test of whether listeners here hear the build go up or down?
