Step 0 · the two things, before anything joins them

A synthesizer and an effect, each on its own

This sequence takes two kinds of instrument that sit in different places on a mixer, one that makes a sound and one that a sound goes through, and tests whether they are one stored field read by different hands. Then it tests a third description, a mathematical way of writing the effect down.

The first thing

A torus wavetable synthesizer

An ordinary wavetable keeps one cycle of a wave, and a reader runs round it to make a tone. Stretch that to two directions: a square of stored heights, called a field. Two phasors pick a point on it. A phasor is a counter that runs round and round at a steady rate, like the wavetable’s reader; one sets how far across, the other how far up. The height under the point is the output, sample after sample. Both counters wrap round, so the square’s left edge meets its right and its top meets its bottom: the shape is a torus, a doughnut. Where the two rates mix, new pitches land at their sums and differences.

Where you meet itLoudon’s 2D Torus Wavetable Synthesizer. It makes sound with nothing coming in.

Step 1 is the field alone; step 2 has two phasors reading it.

The second thing

An effect that reads a field

An effect takes a sound in and sends a sound out. The simplest kind, a waveshaper, keeps one fixed curve and swaps each input value for the curve’s height at that value; it has no memory. Give it a square of heights instead of a curve, and read the square with the input itself: how far across from where the input is now, how far up from where it was a moment ago, a lag τ back. Now what comes out depends on where the signal has been as well as where it is: an effect with memory.

Where you meet itA distortion pedal is its no-lag case: the input reads only along the line where across equals up. The lag is the step past it. It needs a sound coming in.

Step 3 lets the input read the field with no lag; step 4 adds the lag.

The claim that joins them

Both read one stored field; only the hand differs. The synthesizer’s hand is two phasors. The effect’s hand is the input, now and a lag ago. Set the two hands to trace the same path and they give the same samples, to rounding error: one field, one lookup (step 4). The second claim brings in a third description. The standard mathematical way to write an effect with memory is Vito Volterra’s series: a sum of products of the input at chosen lags, the input now times itself, the input now times the input τ ago, and so on, each product with its own weight. The table of those weights is called the kernel. The field-reading effect can be rewritten exactly as a Volterra kernel (step 6).

Three kinds of sameness. Identity: one object, read two ways. Isomorphism: an exact map between two things that stay two. Analogy: a likeness that breaks when you push it.

Two claims, two kinds. Synthesizer and effect: an identity, one field read two ways. Field and kernel: an isomorphism, an exact map between two things that stay two, because the field lives on heights of the signal and the kernel lives on lags.

Where it holds

Synthesizer and effect null wherever the two hands can trace the same path: one pure sine going in, at full level, so that now and a lag ago are two phasors at a ratio of exactly 1 (step 4). Every field read at now and τ ago becomes a kernel, exactly (step 6).

Where it stops

With anything but one pure sine at full level, the effect’s hand wanders where no pair of phasors goes: the field is still one, but the two hands no longer share a path to compare. And the map from field to kernel runs one way only: a kernel with a weight at any other lag has no field of this kind behind it (step 6). Volterra series fit many effects with memory, the ones whose memory fades; an effect with hysteresis, whose memory never fades, is outside them.

Step 1 of 7 · the field alone

A landscape of heights, read along one line

The field is a square of heights: gold where it rises above zero, blue where it dips below. Pick a height up the square and walk straight across it, and the heights under your feet make one curve. Run a cosine back and forth across that line, and the curve shapes the cosine into a tone. The field is built from curves across, one per harmonic: curve m, read by a cosine, gives harmonic m. Moving the line up changes how much of each, so the colour moves.

Step 2 of 7 · the synthesizer's hand

Two phasors, one point on the field

Now give each direction its own hand. One phasor turns at 220 Hz and its shadow sets how far across; a second turns at its own rate and its shadow sets how far up. Together they move one point, which traces a figure across the field. Both phases wrap round, so the pair lives on a torus, and that is the torus synthesizer. Where the two rates mix, new pitches land at their sums and differences.

TryStart on the ring field, which is just across × up. You hear two pitches and neither of them is 220 Hz: that is a ring modulator. Slide the ratio to exactly 2, then 1.5. The figure closes and the partials fall onto one harmonic series. Anywhere in between, the figure takes many turns to close, or never does, and the tone is bell-like.
Step 3 of 7 · the effect's hand, with no lag

Let the input be the hand

Take the phasors away and feed the field a signal. Read it at across = the input and up = the input too. The hand can only stand where across equals up, so it never leaves the diagonal, and the diagonal is one curve. That makes this an ordinary waveshaper, a static curve. A static curve still mixes: give it two tones and it makes their difference and their sum, pitches neither tone has.

Step 4 of 7 · the two hands, through their difference

Read the input twice, and the phasors come back

Now read the input at two moments: across from where it is now, up from where it was a lag τ ago. With one tone in, the moment τ ago is the same cosine, turned back a little. That is two phasors at a ratio of exactly 1, a fixed offset apart. So set the synthesizer to that, flip one hand upside down, and add. If they cancel to silence, the two hands are reading one field along one path.

Step 5 of 7 · what the lag can do

Mixing that depends on which tones went in

Here is a test a static curve cannot pass, even with a filter after it. Send in two tones 30 Hz apart and slide both up together. Their difference stays at 30 Hz the whole way. A static curve makes that 30 Hz tone at one strength, whatever the two tones are, and a filter after it only ever hears 30 Hz, so it can move that strength but never make it rise and fall. With a lag, the two tones are read at two moments, each has turned by its own amount in between, and how strongly they mix depends on the tones themselves.

A static curve then a filter is what the Volterra page calls a Hammerstein structure. That page says it discards intermodulation; step 3 heard it make some. What it cannot make is intermodulation whose strength depends on which tones went in.

Step 6 of 7 · the same reading, written as a kernel

A field of heights, translated onto lags

There is another way to write any effect with memory: a Volterra series. It multiplies the input against itself at chosen lags, and a kernel holds the weight for each choice of lags. The field read at now and τ ago translates into one exactly. Its axes are no longer heights of the signal but lags, and every weight sits at a lag of 0 or τ. The map runs one way only for kernels that sparse: put a weight at any other lag, and no field read at now and τ ago can give it.

The dots on the kernel's own diagonal, at (0, 0) and (τ, τ), are a static curve and its echo: a Hammerstein structure. The two off it, at (0, τ) and (τ, 0), are what made step 5's tone rise and fall; on the ring field they are the only ones.
