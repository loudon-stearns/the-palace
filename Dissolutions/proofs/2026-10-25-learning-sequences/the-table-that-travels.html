The table that travels, step by step

Five short steps up to the proof. A table alone, a loop alone, the two set against each other, a filter alone, then the filter put in the loop. Each step has one thing to hear. Nothing plays until you press Play.

Step 1 of 6 · the table alone

A stored cycle and a reader

A wavetable is one cycle of a wave, kept as a list of numbers, and a reader that runs across it and jumps back to the start. The cycle never changes. The pitch is only how fast the reader goes round: twice as fast, an octave up.

TrySlide the speed and watch the stored cycle hold still while only the reader changes. Then change the shape: the reader is the same, the table is new.
Step 2 of 6 · the loop alone

A delay fed back into itself

Now a different machine. Take a delay line, fill it once, and send its output back into its input. Nothing sweeps across it. The samples travel round the ring and pass a tap that holds still. What went in comes back after one trip, then again, and again. Its length sets the pitch: one trip per period. And a partial lasts only if it turns a whole number of times in one trip, so the loop keeps exactly the harmonic grid: once, twice, three times the pitch, and nothing between.

Hearthe loop, after it was filled once
Left · the ringThe delay line drawn as a circle. Its samples turn past the tap at the top, which never moves. Slowed down for the eye.
Right · what comes outThree trips of what passes the tap. Each trip is the one before it, unchanged.
fill it with
y[n] = y[n − L] fₖ = k × sr ÷ L
(output now) = (the output one trip ago) · (the kth partial) = (a whole number k) × (sample rate) ÷ (loop length in samples): the harmonic grid, with k = 1 the pitch
TryFill it with noise. A burst of hiss, sent round a loop, becomes a steady pitched buzz: the loop remembers one trip and repeats it exactly. Whatever you put in, its length makes it periodic.
Step 3 of 6 · the two, through their difference

Turn one upside down and add them

Fill the loop with the table's own cycle and make the loop exactly one cycle long. Now play both, with one flipped upside down. Where they match, they cancel. Studio engineers call this a null test: if two paths cancel to silence, they are giving you the same numbers. Here the table's moving reader and the loop's moving samples cancel completely.

d[n] = table[n] − loop[n]

TryPlay the difference: silence, for as long as you like. Then make the loop one sample too long and play it again. A one-sample mismatch is enough to make the null speak.
Step 4 of 6 · the filter alone

A filter that only delays

The proof puts one kind of filter in the loop, an allpass. It is a strange filter: it lets every frequency through at full strength, and changes only how late each one arrives. Turn it up and the low frequencies come out later than the high ones. On its own you hear that as a click smeared into a quick downward zap: the highs arrive first.

Heara click, twice a second, through the filter
Left · how late each frequency arrivesThe delay of the proof's filter, eight allpass sections, against frequency. Dashed: how loud each frequency comes out, flat at full strength, always.
Right · the click, after the filterOne click in, what comes out. Flat setting: still a click. Turned up: a chirp, highs first.
H(z) = (a + z⁻¹) ÷ (1 + a × z⁻¹) delay at low frequencies = (1 − a) ÷ (1 + a)
(one allpass section) = ((its coefficient) + (one sample of delay)) ÷ (1 + (its coefficient) × (one sample of delay)) · (how many samples late the lows arrive) = (1 − (coefficient)) ÷ (1 + (coefficient))

To make the smear long enough to hear, the click you play goes through 256 of these sections, thirty-two times the proof's eight. The picture on the left is the proof's eight.

TryStart at the bottom of the slider: a click. Raise it slowly and listen to the click stretch into a zap. Nothing got louder or quieter. Only the timing moved.
Step 5 of 6 · the filter in the loop

Put the filter where only the loop can follow

Now place that filter inside the loop, so every trip goes through it. A table has nowhere to put it: its cycle is fixed, and its reader moves one cycle per period for every harmonic alike. In the loop, each frequency now takes its own time to get round. A partial can only last if it comes back in step with itself, a whole number of turns per trip. Highs take a shorter trip than lows, so they fit their turns at a higher frequency, and the partials walk sharp, off the harmonic grid.

Gold marks · the predictionWhere the round-trip condition says each partial must land.
2π × f × τ + θ(f) = 2π × k
2π × (frequency) × (the bare delay) + (the filter's phase lag at that frequency) = 2π × (a whole number of turns)
TryPlay the loop and raise the filter. The tone starts to shimmer, because its partials no longer line up with each other and keep sliding past. Flip to the table: it cannot follow. Watch the gold marks: the peaks land where the equation sends them.
Step 6 of 6 · the proof

Now the whole thing, on one page

The proof puts every step on one screen. Here is where each one is waiting for you there.

  • The table tab is step 1: the top strip of its readers, a cycle held still with its reader moving.
  • The loop tab is step 2: the lower strip, the samples sliding past a tap that holds still.
  • Their difference tab is step 3: silence, with the measurement saying “0, exactly.”
  • The loop filter slider is steps 4 and 5: the partials leave the dashed grid for the gold marks.
  • One thing the proof adds: Half a sample longer. A loop whose length falls between two samples has to read between them on every trip, and that reading is already a filter. So the identity is exact only for a loop a whole number of samples long, and that edge is where the filter's slot opens.

It ends on a question: is a wavetable a loop with its filter slot left empty, or is the filter what makes the loop a different thing?
