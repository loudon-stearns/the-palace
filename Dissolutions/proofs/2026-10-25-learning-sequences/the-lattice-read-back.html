The lattice read back, step by step

Five short steps up to the proof. One generator, then two, then a ruler laid across the peaks to read them back, then the bound on how large its whole numbers may grow, then the same lattice counted along other steps. Each step has one thing to hear. Nothing plays until you press Play.

Step 1 of 6 · one generator

A harmonic series is rank 1

Start with the plainest tone there is: partials at 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 times one frequency. That frequency is the generator, and every partial is a whole-number multiple of it, a mark on one ruler. One generator, so we call it rank 1. You hear one pitch because the partials share one ruler.

TryTurn off ×1 and listen: the pitch stays where it was. The ruler is still the generator, even with nothing on its first mark. Then light only ×2, ×4 and ×6: the largest ruler that fits jumps an octave, and so does the pitch.
Step 2 of 6 · two generators

Two phases make a lattice

A torus synth keeps a field on a square and reads it with two phases at once: one runs across at g₁, the other up at g₂. Each lit point (m, n) of the field becomes one partial, at m × g₁ + n × g₂. With g₂ ÷ g₁ = √2 the reading line winds round the square and never comes back to where it began, so no one ruler holds the partials. Two generators: rank 2. You hear a bell, not a pitch.

TryPress 3 ÷ 2. The blue line closes after two turns, and the bell pulls together into a pitch: every partial is now a whole multiple of 55 Hz. The synth still turns two phases, and the sound is rank 1.
Step 3 of 6 · reading back

Which whole numbers explain the peaks?

Now turn round. The analysis gets the sound and nothing else. It finds the peaks in the spectrum, then lays a ruler across them: a trial generator with a mark at every whole multiple. A peak is explained if it sits within half a hertz of a mark. What the ruler explains can be played back; what it cannot is left over in the difference.

TryStart on the harmonic tone at 110 Hz: every peak on a mark, and what it leaves is silence. Switch to the bell. The 110 Hz ruler catches the bottom row and nothing else; play what it leaves and you hear the partials it missed. Press 155.56 Hz and g₂'s own multiples land instead. No single ruler with small whole numbers takes the lot.
Step 4 of 6 · the bound on the whole numbers

How large may the whole numbers grow?

Rulers can always get finer. Shrink the generator far enough and its marks crowd so close that every peak finds one. So the analysis has to be told how large a whole number it may use, and the rank it hands back depends on that bound. Set the bound low and this sound needs two generators. Raise it past one point and a single tiny ruler explains every peak, and tells you almost nothing.

Bottom · rank against the boundThe rank the analysis gives at each bound, from 4 to 200. The gold line is where you are. Nothing in the sound changes along it.
the sound
rank = the fewest generators whose whole-number sums, none above K, land within 0.5 Hz of every peak
(the rank) = how many generators the sound needs, once you say (how large the whole numbers may grow) and (how close a peak must land)

Each peak may miss its mark by 0.5 Hz here, the proof's default. Measure more finely and the tiny ruler must shrink further, but for any finite set of peaks at any finite precision it is mathematically always there. This step tries every single ruler, not only those that put a peak exactly on a mark; when none fits, it runs the proof's own two-generator search, which keeps its whole numbers to 8 or less.

TryOn 7 ÷ 5, slide the bound down from 40. Below 27 the rank jumps from 1 to 2, and nothing in the sound changed. Then pick √2 and push the bound up to 65: one ruler at 9.16 Hz fits every peak. Play the sound over that ruler: about nine ticks a second, which is nobody's idea of what that bell is.
Step 5 of 6 · the same lattice, counted along other steps

The analysis gets the lattice, not the hand

Two generators draw a lattice, but they are not the only two that draw it. Swap g₂ for g₁ + g₂ and every point you lit still has whole-number coordinates, different ones, and lands on exactly the same frequency. The sound cannot tell the two apart, so neither can the analysis: it recovers the lattice, not the generators that drew it. Any whole-number change of steps does the same, as long as its determinant is 1 or −1.

TryPlay the difference: silence, for every basis with determinant ±1. Then pick g₁, 2 × g₂, determinant 2. Points on odd rows now need half a step, there is no such thing, and rounded to the nearest whole number they land on the wrong frequency. The null speaks.
Step 6 of 6 · the proof

Now the whole round trip, on one page

The proof plays a field through two generators, hands only the sound to the analysis, and draws what comes back. Here is where each step is waiting for you there.

  • One generator and two generators, steps 1 and 2, are The synthesis tab with its Bell, Two rows and Diagonal fields, the g₂ ÷ g₁ slider and its presets. The middle panel is the field's points; the bottom panel is the reading, each row slid along by n × g₂.
  • The ruler, step 3, is the right panel, what comes out: the measured spectrum and the peaks the analysis found. The tolerance slider is the half hertz you were forgiven.
  • The bound, step 4, is the whole numbers up to slider and the 7 ÷ 5 preset. The measurement box says, at √2, that harmonic numbers up to 157 would make the same peaks rank 1 on a 3.79 Hz fundamental. That 157 and 3.79 Hz come from the proof's search, which only tries rulers that pin one of the low peaks exactly on a mark; as step 4 showed, a ruler already fits at 65.
  • Another basis, step 5, is the Diagonal field. The read back panel draws the points in the generators the analysis found, and the measurement's last line writes the drawing generators in them: g₁ = ĝ₁, g₂ = −ĝ₁ + ĝ₂.
  • What the proof adds: the two-generator search itself, which builds its candidates from the peaks and the gaps between them and keeps the simplest whole numbers; The analysis, played and Their difference, the found lattice played back with each partial's loudness and phase measured from the sound; and points you can light or dark by clicking the field.

The Rank-N Lattice Analysis page, as it stands, calls rank a property of the source and has the analysis hand back the generators. Steps 4 and 5 show otherwise: the rank depends on how large the whole numbers may grow, and what comes back is the lattice, which more than one pair of generators draws. Offer B in the Dissolutions baton is the change that would bring the page in line.

It ends on a question: is the rank a property of the sound, or of the sound and how much you are willing to count?
