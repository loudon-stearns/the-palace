Dissolutions · learning sequence · Phase Reduction

The shadow that locks, step by step

Six short steps up to the proof. One oscillator and the loop it keeps falling back onto, a kick and when it lands, the same curve read off the loop itself, two oscillators that lock, why their pitch climbs, and the phase-only shadow. Each step has one thing to hear or see. Nothing plays until you press Play.

Step 1 of 7 · one oscillator alone

An oscillator that keeps its own shape

Here is one oscillator, the van der Pol: a position x, a speed y, and a rule that pushes them round. Start it anywhere and it falls onto the same closed loop, the gold one, and goes round it for good. Where it is on the loop is its phase, θ: one number. What you hear is x, going round 220 times a second.

Keep an eye on the last term, − x. It is a spring: it pulls the position back toward the middle, harder the farther out it is. It comes back in step 5.

TrySwitch the loop. The gentle one is nearly a circle and sounds nearly pure; the relaxation loop is a lopsided box and buzzes. Watch the blue dots each time: wherever they start, they end on the gold line.
Step 2 of 7 · a kick alone

Kick it, and it comes back early or late

Now kick it: add a little to its speed at one moment. The loop pulls it back on, but not to where it would have been. It comes back a little ahead or a little behind, and stays that way, because nothing along the loop pulls it back. How far, against when in the cycle the kick lands, is the phase response curve: the PRC.

Right · the PRCBlue dots: kick it by 0.01 at each phase, let it settle, and measure how far ahead (up) or behind (down) it ends, per unit of kick. The line marks your phase.
Δθ = Z(θ) × Δy f_kicked ÷ f = 1 ÷ (1 − Z(θ) × Δy ÷ 2π)
(how far ahead it ends up) = (the PRC at the phase of the kick) × (the size of the kick) · (kicked pitch) ÷ (pitch) = 1 ÷ (1 − (what each kick gains) ÷ (one whole cycle))

One kick only moves the phase, which the ear can’t hear on its own. So the sound kicks it by 0.3 once every cycle, at the same phase. Each kick gains the same bit, and the gains add up to a change of pitch.

TryPlay both together and slide the kick round the loop. Where the curve sits above the line the kicked one runs sharp and the two beat against each other; near where it crosses zero the beating slows almost to nothing; below it, the kicked one runs flat.
Step 3 of 7 · the PRC without kicking

The same curve, read off the loop itself

You can get the PRC without kicking at all. Follow one trip round the loop and watch what it does to every small nudge: across the loop a nudge shrinks, and along the loop it comes back exactly the size it left, a multiplier of 1. Read the direction that goes with that 1, its left eigenvector, at each phase, and you have the PRC. That is Floquet theory, and the proof’s first claim: the gold line and the blue dots are one curve.

The gap shrinks as the kick does. The curve describes small kicks; a big one knocks the oscillator far enough off the loop that the curve is no longer the whole story.

TrySet the kick to 0.1, then 0.03, then 0.01, and watch the dots close onto the gold line. Then switch the loop. The curve changes shape with it: the shape of the loop designs the PRC.
Step 4 of 7 · two oscillators, coupled

Two oscillators, joined, lock together

Now two of them, the second tuned a little sharp, and join them: each one’s speed gets a nudge of ε times the gap between their positions. With no coupling the gap between their phases slides round and round, and they drift. Give them a little and the gap stops sliding: they lock. On this loop they lock far apart, heading toward opposite as the coupling grows, one near the top of its swing while the other is near the bottom.

TryStart at no coupling, both in the middle, and hear the slow swell as they slide past each other. Raise the coupling until the trail stands up straight: locked far apart, the two partly cancel and it gets quieter. Push the detuning out until it slants again: too far apart for this much coupling. Keep half an ear on the pitch, too. It moves. That is the next step.
Step 5 of 7 · why the pitch climbs

The coupling is a spring, and it stiffens the swing

The coupling pulls each one toward the other’s position, ε times the distance between them: a spring between the two. Locked nearly opposite, they are always far apart, so the spring is always stretched, and it always pulls each one back toward the middle, the same way as its own − x. A stiffer pull back means a faster swing, so the pitch rises. It is the higher mode of two pendulums joined by a spring, and it is not an error.

ε × (x₂ − x₁) ≈ −2 × ε × x₁ when x₂ ≈ −x₁ f ÷ f₀ ≈ √(1 + ε × (1 − cos ψ))
(the spring’s pull on the first) = (coupling) × ((the other’s position) − (its own)) ≈ −2 × (coupling) × (its own position), when the two are opposite · (new pitch) ÷ (old pitch) ≈ √(1 + (coupling) × (1 − cos (the gap)))

The count treats each swing as a circle, which the van der Pol loop is not, so here it lands a few points under the measured pitch. On the gentle loop, which is nearly round, it lands within a point of it at every coupling from 0.1 to 0.5.

TryPlay the pair and pull the coupling from 0 up to 0.5: the pitch climbs more than six semitones. Flip to one alone to hear where it started. Then start them together and play again: the spring goes slack, and the pitch comes home.
Step 6 of 7 · the phases alone

Forget everything but the phases

Phase reduction makes a bet. If the loop pulls each oscillator back much faster than the coupling pushes it off, then knowing where each one is around its loop is nearly knowing everything. So keep only the two phases, and pull each with Γ: the PRC from steps 2 and 3, times the other’s push, averaged over a cycle. That is Kuramoto’s equation, the shadow, and it locks where the curve on the left crosses zero going down.

The shadow hears the spring too, but only to first order: its pitch is 1 + ε × Γ(ψ) ÷ ω, a straight line in the coupling. At strong coupling it runs sharp of the real pair.

TryAt 0.04 on the van der Pol loop, play the oscillators, then the shadow: they sound alike, and their difference is quiet. Raise the coupling to 0.5 and play the difference: the shadow’s pitch has run ahead. Then pick the gentle loop at 0.04: the oscillators lock, and the shadow says they drift.
Step 7 of 7 · the proof

Now the whole thing, on one page

The proof puts every step on one screen and runs the full pair and the shadow side by side. Here is where each step is waiting for you there.

  • The loop buttons, Gentle, Van der Pol and Relaxation, are step 1, and the gold loop in the top-left panel.
  • The phase response curve under the measurement box is steps 2 and 3: blue dots from kicks of 0.01, the gold line from the eigenvector. The box’s first two lines are step 3’s multipliers and gap.
  • The oscillators tab, the coupling and detuning sliders, and the bottom-left reader are step 4: a vertical line of dots means locked.
  • Step 5 is in the same tab. Raise the coupling and the pitch climbs. The proof doesn’t say why; it is the spring.
  • Their phase shadow and Their difference tabs, and the dashed line in the reader, are step 6. The box’s fourth line says where each one locks.
  • What the proof adds: the tongue, bottom right. It runs the full pair at every detuning and coupling on a grid, gold where it locks, with blue lines where the shadow says locking ends. At weak coupling the gold sits between the lines; turn the coupling up and it walks away from them. The box’s third line weighs step 6’s bet: how fast the loop pulls back, against how hard the coupling pushes.

It ends on a question. The PRC half is exact. The shadow half holds only as the coupling goes to zero, and how small is small enough depends on the loop. Should Phase Reduction stay in the catalogue as an identity, stand as an isomorphism, or be split into the exact half and the shadow?
