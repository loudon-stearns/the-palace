The shadow that locks

Two oscillators, each a loop it keeps falling back onto, nudge each other through a weak coupling. Phase reduction says you can forget everything about them except where each one is around its loop: one number apiece, a phase, and the two phases obey Kuramoto’s equation. Here the full oscillators and their phase-only shadow run side by side. Turn the coupling up and find where the shadow stops following.

The question this asks the page

The PRC claim is exact: the two curves are one, closer the smaller the kick. The Kuramoto claim is not. On the van der Pol loop at weak coupling the shadow locks where the oscillators lock, at nearly the same gap; turn the coupling up and the gold cells walk away from the lines. Then try the gentle loop, where even weak coupling outruns the shadow. Should a dissolution that holds only in a limit, a limit whose size depends on the loop, stay in the catalogue as an identity, stand as an isomorphism, or be split into the exact half and the shadow?

Two lines, because the page makes two claims. The PRC is Floquet data in the strict sense: kick-measured and eigenvector-computed curves agree, and agree better in step with smaller kicks. Kuramoto as the shadow of a population of limit cycles is a map that keeps the structure (who locks, at what gap) and keeps it exactly only in the limit of weak coupling. That is the isomorphism the baton predicted, and an asymptotic one. On a nearly round loop, where this coupling’s averaged pull almost cancels, the limit is so tight that the shadow misses locking at couplings the van der Pol loop shrugs off. Whether it stays a member is Loudon’s question. This is the maker’s verdict; your sit may overturn it.
