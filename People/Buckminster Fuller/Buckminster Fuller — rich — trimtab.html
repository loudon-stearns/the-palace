A rudder for the rudder

The page says the whole of him is one well-placed intervention rather than more force, and that is the trimtab. Here is a ship to try it on: a heavy hull, a rudder the water loads, and a small tab on the rudder's trailing edge. Five steps, one thing to see in each; the last measures the leverage and the delay it costs. Every number on this sheet is this model's, not a real ship's and not Fuller's. Nothing here makes a sound.

The rule, stated before the run Both arms make the same 20° turn. The autopilot steers the tab arm: it moves only the tab, and the water swings the rudder. The direct arm moves the rudder itself, along the exact path that gives the ship the same push, so heading and time match by construction. Effort is the torque the hand's gear supplies, summed over the 90-second run, E = ∫|Q| dt, because holding a loaded rudder costs effort even when nothing moves. Leverage L = E direct ÷ E tab. L ≤ 1 means no leverage, and so does a tab arm that never settles within 0.5° of 20° inside the 90 seconds. Either one is the page wrong at this placement, in this model; L > 1 with the turn made is the page right. Nothing is guessed here, so there is no chance level. The delay is read off the same run: the tab arm's settling time minus that of a direct arm steered by the same autopilot through the same gear speed. One null first: the matched arm's arithmetic, run on the direct arm's own path, must give back the direct arm's own effort within 1%. How it can fail. Slide the tab forward of 0.90 m and the turn is not made, for three different reasons the verdict names: the tab runs out of its 30° travel (0.60 to 0.90 m), its leverage is truly at or below 1 (0.44 to 0.60 m), or its own side force outpushes the rudder and the ship turns the wrong way (forward of 0.44 m). And know what L is before you read it: it equals the steady leverage the model's lever arms set (step 4), so this run tests the delay, not the leverage.
