The grid holds still. Can you see the hand drift?

The paragraph above makes a claim about seeing: a hand-ruled line drifts by a hair, a grid held still lets you see the hair, and without the grid the hair is lost in noise. This walk draws its own lines from a stated model of a hand, starts with one of them beside a true line, and ends on a blind test you take, where the only thing that changes between the two halves is the grid.

One line by hand, one true

The top line is true: straight to the pixel. The bottom one is ruled by a model hand that wanders a little as it goes and lands back on its mark at the end. The orange trace under them is that wander, magnified twenty times, so you can see its shape even when the line itself looks straight. Slide the drift down until you can no longer tell which line is which without the trace.

The hand's drift, stated

Every hand-ruled line here comes from one rule, so the test can say exactly what it is hiding.

yi(x) = ȳi + a · wi(x),   wi(0) = wi(L) = 0,   rms(wi) = 1
In words: line i sits where the ruler says, ȳi, plus the hand's drift. The drift wi is a random walk, one step per pixel, smoothed over about 24 pixels so it wanders slowly like a hand and not a tremor, pinned to zero at both ends, and scaled so its typical distance from the true line, the root mean square, is exactly a pixels. Each line has its own walk, seeded by the trial and its row, so no two lines fall the same.

Here are ten of them, each one's drift magnified twenty times on its own dotted baseline. Same rule, same amplitude, ten different hands.

Step 1 is one of these beside a true line, at full size.

The grid holds still

Ten hand-ruled lines inside a grid that does not move: evenly spaced, starting on one edge, crossed by true vertical rules every 48 pixels. Switch between straight and drifting at the same amplitude and watch where the difference shows: in the gaps between neighbours, and at each crossing, where a cell is a hair taller on one side than the other.

Set drift to 1 px and switch to straight: that is step 1's true line, ten times.

Take it away

The same ten lines, the same drift, line for line. The slider takes away only the grid: the even spacing, the shared left edge and the vertical rules fade toward a scatter of lines at uneven heights and offsets. The lines stay horizontal, the same length and the same weight, because a tilted line is drawn on different pixels, and that would change something other than the grid. Under the field the piece measures its own pixels, so you can see the drift is the same in both, by the number.

What taking it away leaves. The open half still has the field's straight border, the horizontal, the pixel rows every line runs along, and each line's own straight chord between its pinned ends; it adds no noise. In both halves no line comes nearer the border than 46 px, the nearest line is exactly as far from the border in the two halves of each field, and scattered lines are never closer together than the grid's 32 px. So the test asks a narrower question than the page: does the grid's order (even spacing, a shared edge, cross-rules) help you see the drift, when everything else stays? It cannot reach "vanishes into noise", because nothing here is noise.

Slide back to 100% to return to step 3.

The test, blind

What you do. 64 fields, one at a time. Each shows ten lines, held in the grid or scattered without it. On each field the lines are either all drifting, at one of four amplitudes (0.25, 0.5, 1 or 2 px root mean square), or all perfectly straight, chosen by a fair coin for that field alone. You answer straight or drifting (keys S and D work too). Take as long as you like. No answer is marked until the end. Please leave the zoom as it is: zooming magnifies the drift, and the piece notes it if it changes.

Chance, before the first trial. A guess is right half the time. Each half, grid and no grid, gets 8 fields at each amplitude, 32 in all, so a guesser scores about 16 of 32 in each. A half is above chance at 21 of 32 or more; a guesser gets there about 5.5% of the time.

The verdict rule, before the first trial. The page's claim holds for you if, with the grid, you get at least 6 more of your 32 right than without it, and at least 21 of 32. It goes the other way if the half without the grid wins by the same rule. Anything closer is no clear difference. If neither half reaches 21, the drift was too faint to tell anything; if both reach 30, too easy to tell anything. The page's strong form, "the difference vanishes into noise", is out of this test's reach: the open half keeps the frame, the horizontal and the pixel rows and adds no noise (step 4). The piece still marks it when the claim holds and you saw no amplitude at all without the grid (an amplitude counts as seen at 7 of its 8 right), but that says the grid's order mattered to you, not that noise swallowed the drift. Both halves are scored, and hits and false alarms are shown for each.

What the rule can and cannot see. A reader guessing every field gets a verdict for the page about 2.9% of the time. The page's claim is a strong one, and the test is sized for it: a model reader whom the grid helps by two doublings of amplitude gets "holds" roughly two times in three to three in four, depending on the reader model (63–69% on one, 75% on another); one whom it helps by a single doubling gets it only 22–31% of the time, and otherwise "no clear difference"; one whom it does not help at all gets "holds" at most about 6.5%, depending on how sharp their eyes are. The numbers come from the check beside this piece and the challenger's, both on this piece's own scoring code.

Every line here is drawn by this piece from the rule in step 2; no painting of hers is reproduced, and the model hand is a model, not a measurement of anyone's hand. Lines are not stroked by the browser: each is laid on the pixels as a band of fixed width, so a straight line and a drifting one carry the same ink in every column and the same darkest pixel, and only their position differs (the meter shows it, and Agnes Martin — rich — check.mjs re-checks it headless: on 2026-11-04, at 1, 1.25 and 2 device pixels per field pixel, every drifting line read back within 0.6% of the amplitude asked, every straight line at 0.00000 px, each line's drift the same with the grid and without it to 0.014 px, and on the 8-bit pixels the canvas shows, a straight line and a 0.25 px drifting one within 0.3% of each other in total ink). The piece can measure its own pixels; it cannot see. The threshold that answers the page is yours.
