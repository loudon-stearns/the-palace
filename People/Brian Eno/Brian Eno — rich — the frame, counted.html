How much of the drift was chosen?

The section says Eno chooses the loops, the sounds, the constraints and the take, then lets go inside the box he built, so "the system did it" quietly credits the system for his selecting. Here the same kind of loop machine is built with every choice counted, and then a coin is put in it, to see what it takes for the system to decide more than the person who framed it. The sounds are made by this file. Silent until you press something.

One chosen length

Before a loop can drift, someone cuts it. Choose a length. The slider offers every length from 2 to 40 seconds in sixteenths of a second, so picking one is picking one of 609. A fair way to say how big that choice is: count the yes/no questions it would take someone to find out which length you picked, halving the options each time.

size of a choice = log2(number of options)   questions
(how big a choice is) = how many yes/no questions it takes to pin down which option was picked. One of 2 is one question; one of 609 is about 9¼. A finer slider makes the same choice bigger; a coarser one, smaller.

The whole frame

A piece of loops needs more than one length. How many loops, each one's length, each one's note, and the sound they are sung in. That is the frame: everything that is decided before the machine runs. Build one, play it, and watch the tally. Every row is a choice a person made.

Let it run, and ask any note where it came from

The test: who made this hour?

The section's reading is that the system only sounds free because someone chose the frame. With loops alone, step 3 already shows every start traced to a choice. The reading can fail, though, and this is how: put a coin in the machine. Each time a loop comes round, the coin decides whether that note sounds. That is Cage's move, the one the section sets against Eno's. Now the machine is making decisions of its own. Then add the take: Eno chose which stretch of tape to keep, so say how many takes you heard before you kept one. The bar counts both sides for one hour.

At "always sounds" this is step 3's hour exactly; the frame is step 2's.

H(p) = − p log2 p − (1 − p) log2(1 − p)   questions per start
(how much the coin decides each time a loop comes round) = the yes/no questions it takes to learn how the coin fell, where p is the chance the note sounds. A coin that always says yes decides nothing; a fair coin decides one question a start.
the system's share = (starts in the hour × H(p)) ⁄ (that + the frame + log2(takes heard))
(how much of the hour the system decided) = what the coin decided, over everything anyone or anything decided.

The counts depend on the menus: a finer slider makes every choice of yours bigger, and the coin's count grows with every minute the hour runs. What does not depend on any menu is the first result: with ideal loops, the machine adds nothing to what the frame determines, as any machine without chance would. The piece counts determination, not foresight or taste: it cannot say whether anyone foresaw a given chord, which choices were good, or what you hear. And it counts ideal loops. Real tape stretches and wavers, and the room colours what it plays; that is unchosen variation the count leaves out, and by its own terms it would be the system's.
