He set the lengths. What did the lengths do?

The section says Music for Airports is tape loops of different lengths drifting out of phase. This builds that machine from one loop up and then asks it a question: are different lengths enough to make a piece that never comes round again? The notes and the sound are this piece's own; from Eno it takes only three loop lengths he once gave in a talk. Silent until you press something.

One loop

A tape loop is a strip of tape spliced into a ring. Put one sung note on it and leave the rest blank: every time the splice comes round, the note sounds again. One loop is a clock. Set its length and press play; the ring turns once per length, and the note sounds each time the playhead passes the top.

One loop alone repeats exactly, for ever. Whatever makes Eno's piece change, it is not inside any single loop.

Two loops, drifting

Now two loops of different lengths, each with its own note, started together. Each is still a clock; what changes is the gap between them. The square shows both at once: across is where loop A is in its turn, up is where loop B is, so one moment is one point, and time draws a line. If the line closes up after a few passes, the pair repeats. If it keeps finding new ground, the pair is drifting. Try the three pairs.

Each loop is step 1's. Set B to A's length and you are back to one clock.

4 and 8 are different lengths and never drift: every second note of A lands on B. 23.5 and 25.875 are two of the lengths Eno gave (the talk is cited on the last step); this pair comes back exactly after 81 minutes.

Many loops

Add loops, up to seven, each with its length and its note. Each pair drifts as in step 2; together they make chords that form and dissolve as the starts slide past each other. The test: are different lengths enough?

The section's line is "he set the lengths, the room made the piece". If the lengths are what he set, they alone decide when the piece repeats, and different lengths should keep it from repeating. Here four sets are measured two ways: when the whole comes back exactly, and when every loop's start lands within a small tolerance of the others again, which is what an ear would hear as the same moment returning. Choose the tolerance; play any row.

The exact column is step 3's lcm. The ear column relaxes it: the starts need only be close, not equal (their spread at most ε, so a return can sit right on the edge). The last column counts every such return in the first hour, one by one.

(when it sounds as if it has come round) = the first moment at which every loop has made a whole number of turns, give or take ε. Found by trying each turn of the longest loop in order, up to four million turns.

The lengths 23.5, 25.875 and 29.9375 s are from Eno's talk "Generative Music" (San Francisco, 8 June 1996, printed in In Motion Magazine), where he gives them with "or something like that" and calls the cycles incommensurable, "not likely to come back into sync again". Real tape stretches and wavers, so a real loop's length is never exact; the ear column is the more honest of the two. What this piece cannot check is what you hear. The table measures starts, not music.
