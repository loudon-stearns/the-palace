Loss per trip

A waveguide string has no friction term. What it loses, it loses going round its loop: a fraction each trip, and more from the high partials than the low ones if the loop has a filter in it. Here the string is played, and every partial's ring time is read back off the sound. Nothing plays until you press pluck.

Step 1 of 4 · one partial

A wave that keeps a fraction g each time round

Start with one partial alone: a sine at 220 Hz. Each trip round the loop takes one period, and each trip it keeps a fraction g of itself. A fixed fraction each trip is a fixed number of decibels each trip, so the level falls in a straight line, and the ring time is just how many trips it takes to lose 60 dB.

Step 2 of 4 · every partial at once

A pluck holds every partial, and gain alone treats them all alike

Now a real pluck: a burst of noise in a delay line one period long, going round and round. The loop makes it a string: only waves that fit the loop survive, the partials at 220, 440, 660 Hz and up. With only the gain in the loop, every partial keeps the same fraction each trip. They fade together, and the note never darkens. It sounds like a buzz that gets quieter.

Step 3 of 4 · a Q for every partial

A filter in the loop takes more from the top

Put a gentle low-pass in the loop: each trip, mix a little of the sample before into the sample now. Low partials barely notice; high ones lose a little more every trip. Each partial is a resonator of its own, and now each has its own Q. The upper partials die first, and the note goes from bright to dark. Set the filter to 0 and you are back on step 2.

Step 4 of 4 · the test

Brightness and sustain are one curve

Every partial's loss each trip, measured off the sound, against what the page says it should be: g times what the filter keeps. If the page is right, the gain lifts the whole curve by the same decibels at every partial, which is mostly sustain, and the filter bends only its upper end, which is mostly brightness. Two hands, one curve.
