Who keeps the time

Galileo watched one ringer swing a great bell and decided a pendulum moves only at its own rate. Young showed it moves at the rate of a smooth push. Both were watching something true. Here both pushers work the same weight, slowed 300 times so the eye can follow; the sound is the same run at full speed. Nothing plays until you press start.

Step 1 of 4 · Galileo's ringer

A ringer who watches the bell

One ringer, one rope. He gives one tug to start it, then waits, and tugs again each time the bell comes back past the middle toward him. He has no clock. His timing is the bell's. The bell has some friction, so each tug only makes up what the last swing lost, and the swing grows until they balance.

Step 2 of 4 · push times speed

The moment of the tug decides whether it gives or takes

Now let him wait for a different point in the swing. A tug while the bell is coming toward him, fast, adds the most. A tug at the top of the swing, where it stands still for an instant, adds almost nothing. A tug while it is moving away takes energy out. What a push gives is the push times the speed it meets. Set his moment to 0° and you are back on step 1.

Watch the third box too: tugs anywhere but the middle nudge the rate as well, slower the later he waits. Galileo's ringer pulled in time, at the middle.

Step 3 of 4 · a push with its own clock

A push that keeps its own time

Now the other pusher. He does not watch the bell. He pushes smoothly, back and forth, at a rate of his own: Young's push. At first the bell does both, its own ring and his rhythm, beating against each other. The ring dies away, as friction makes it, and what is left moves at his rate. Under the traces, green is energy going in; orange is energy the bell hands back to him.

TryPress start at half its rate and watch the first swings beat, then settle into his rhythm. Read the second box: well off its own rate, most of what goes in each cycle comes back out. Slide his rate onto the bell's own and the orange disappears.

Step 4 of 4 · the test

Every push goes in and none comes back

The section's claims, each measured on a run: pushed, it moves at the push's rate; at its own rate it swings Q times as far, a quarter cycle behind, and no energy comes back; the push only ever supplies what friction spends; the peak slides below its own rate as friction grows, and below Q 0.707 it is gone. And the ringer, beside it: his tugs keep the bell's rate.

TrySlide Q down past 0.707 and watch the peak flatten into a push held still. Then put his rate on the bell's own and press start: you hear the swell build at its own pitch. Who keeps the time is the whole difference: the ringer takes his from the bell, so the bell keeps its own; Young's pusher brings his own, so the bell takes his.
