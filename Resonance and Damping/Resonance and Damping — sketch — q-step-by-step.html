Resonance and Damping · the way up to Q you can hear

Q, step by step

Five short steps up to the proof. A thing that rings, the same thing forgetting, a push at someone else's rate, a push near its own rate, and the moment of the push. Each step is something whole you can play, and each keeps the one before it inside: put a control back to its end and you are on the step before. Nothing plays until you press Pluck or Play.

Step 1 of 6 · its own rate

A weight on a spring rings at one rate

Hang a weight on a spring and flick it. It bobs up and down at one rate, and the rate is its own: flick it harder and it bobs further, not faster. A stiffer spring pulls back harder, so it bobs faster. A heavier weight is harder to turn round, so it bobs slower. Here there is no friction at all, so once flicked it never stops.

the weight, flicked once, with nothing to slow it
Left · the weightSlowed down 300 times so the eye can follow it. The pitch you hear is the same motion at full speed. The block grows with its weight; the wire thickens with its stiffness.
Right · where it has beenIts height over the last few seconds, newest on the right. Every swing as tall as the last.
f = √(k ÷ m) ÷ 2π
(how many bobs a second) = (the square root of stiffness over weight) ÷ (one turn of the circle)
TryMake the spring four times as stiff and listen: one octave up. Make the weight four times as heavy: one octave down. Do both and you are back where you started, because only stiffness over weight counts. Then flick it a few times while it is already moving. The swing gets bigger or smaller, but the rate never changes.
Step 2 of 6 · forgetting

Add friction and the ring dies

Now the same spring in air, or in honey. Friction takes a little energy every swing, so each swing is a little smaller than the last, until the weight hangs still. How fast it forgets is one number. Audio people call it Q: high Q, long ring; low Q, a short thud. Slide Q all the way up to “none” and you are back on step 1.

the same weight, now with friction
Left · the weightSlowed 300 times, so a ring that lasts a tenth of a second here takes thirty.
Right · one pluck, at full speedHatched: how tall the swings still are. Red: where they have fallen 60 dB, about a thousandth of where they started.
Q = 1 ÷ 2ζ ring time ≈ 2.2 × Q ÷ f
(Q) = one over twice the damping · (seconds to fall 60 dB) ≈ 2.2 × (Q) ÷ (bobs a second)
TryCount the swings. Double Q and the ring lasts twice as long, at the same pitch. Then drop Q below 1: it stops ringing and just sags back, a thud with no note in it.
Step 3 of 6 · someone else's rate

Push it, and it moves at your rate

Stop flicking and start pushing, smoothly, up and down, at a rate that is not its own. For a moment it does both: its own ring, and your push. Then its own ring dies away, as step 2 said it would, and what is left moves at your rate. Galileo thought this was impossible. Thomas Young showed in 1807 that it is not.

TryPress Stop, then Play, to start the push again from rest and watch the first seconds: two rates beating against each other, then one. Move the push rate and the swing follows it. Its own rate only decides how far it swings, which is the next step.
Step 4 of 6 · steps 2 and 3 together

Push near its own rate and the push piles up

Push a swing at the swing's own rate and every push lands on top of the last. Push at any other rate and some pushes fight it. So how far it swings depends on how close your rate is to its own, and friction (Q, from step 2) decides how much. Right at its own rate it swings Q times as far as the same push held still. The band it answers to is f ÷ Q wide. Noise is every push at once, so noise through it comes out as a pitch.

The marksAmber: your push. Hatched: the band f ÷ Q wide where it answers most.
height at f = Q width = f ÷ Q
(how many times further than a held push, at its own rate) = (Q) · (the band it answers) = (its rate) ÷ (Q)
TrySweep at Q 4, then at Q 60. The higher Q, the taller and narrower the peak, and the longer the swell takes to build: the narrow peak and the long ring of step 2 are one number. Then choose every push at once and raise Q: a pitch comes out of the hiss.
Step 5 of 6 · the moment of the push

At its own rate, the swing is a quarter cycle behind

Watch when the weight is highest against when you push hardest. Push slowly and they move together: the weight just follows your hand. Push fast and they move against each other. At its own rate the weight is a quarter cycle behind, and that is the moment it is moving fastest: you push it while it is already going your way, so every push adds and none takes back. That is why the push piles up.

a sine push; the lag measured from the weight
Left · push and swingAmber: the push. Ink: where the weight is. Green, when shown: how fast it is moving.
Right · the lagHow far behind the push the weight is, for every push rate. The dot is yours.
lag = atan2(u ÷ Q, 1 − u²), u = f_push ÷ f
(how far behind) goes from none, to a quarter cycle exactly at its own rate, to half a cycle far above it; Q sets how suddenly it turns
TrySet the push to exactly its own rate and show its speed: the green speed line and the amber push rise and fall together. Then raise Q and sweep slowly past its own rate: the lag turns over almost at once.
Step 6 of 6 · the proof

Now the whole thing, on one page

Q you can hear puts every step on one screen, as a digital resonator: two numbers, f and Q. Here is where each step is waiting for you there.

  • A strike is steps 1 and 2: the ring, and the red line where it has fallen 60 dB.
  • A sine push is step 3, and sweep the push through the peak is step 4.
  • Noise is step 4's every push at once.
  • The lag scope is step 5, measured live from the sound.
  • One thing the proof adds: the pole radius r. In a computer the weight and spring become one line of arithmetic, and r is how much of the ring is kept each sample. That line is the resonance knob on a synth filter.

It ends on a question for you: where in your own instruments is something ringing that you have been treating as just a knob?
