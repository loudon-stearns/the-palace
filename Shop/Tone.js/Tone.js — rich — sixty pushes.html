Sixty pushes a second: enough to hear the lock?

The Gotcha says two sines coupled by Kuramoto's equation can be driven from the animation frame: step the equation about sixty times a second, push both frequencies with setValueAtTime, and you hear the beating slow and stop, with no per-sample worklet. Tone can't load here, so this walks the claim under Tone, in plain Web Audio: the page's method beside the same equation run once per sample. Five steps; the test is the fourth, and the fifth is for your ears.

Two sines, no coupling

Two sine tones, A at 220 Hz and B a little above it. Added together they swell and fade: the beat, as many times a second as they are hertz apart. Nothing pulls them together yet, so the gap between their phases just turns, steadily.

Δf = the sum swells and fades times a second

Couple them, the page's way

dθA/dt = 2π · ( fA + K · sin(θB − θA) ) , dθB/dt = 2π · ( fB + K · sin(θA − θB) )
(how fast each tone's phase turns) = 2π × ( its own frequency + coupling strength × sin(how far the other is ahead of it) ) — each tone speeds up when the other is ahead and slows when it is behind. K is in hertz, as in the shipped explorer.
Kc = |fB − fA| ⁄ 2 , beat = √( Δf² − (2K)² ) below it, and none above
(the coupling that locks them) = half the gap between their frequencies; below it they still beat, but slower, at the square root of (gap squared − (twice the coupling) squared); at and above it they share one frequency and the beat stops.

Driven as the Gotcha says: once per animation frame the page steps the equation and pushes both frequencies with setValueAtTime. Raise K slowly from zero and listen for the beat to slow down on the way to Kc, and stop past it.

Kc = beat predicted = beat in the model = —silent until you play

Two ways to drive it

setValueAtTime does not draw a curve at the sample rate. It holds each value until the next push: a staircase at the frame rate. Web Audio renders that staircase sample by sample, and the oscillator turns it into a phase with no jumps. The other way runs the equation itself once per sample, in an AudioWorklet, and the two tones really pull on each other. Switch between them at the same K; switching starts both tones again from the same phase.

Try: K = 6, then 30 pushes a second. Past K · cos φ* ≈ rate ⁄ 2π the frame step is too coarse for the coupling, and the lock shudders at half the push rate. At 60 pushes that edge is K ≈ 9.6 Hz, past this slider; at 30 it is 4.9, at 20 it is 3.4. A browser saving power can hold a page to 30 frames a second, and the shipped explorer lets its step grow to 1/20 s before it clamps.

The test, measured

Both ways rendered offline (OfflineAudioContext, 48 kHz), where a push every 1/60 s lands exactly on schedule — the page's method at its best. Each tone is rendered on its own channel and the phase gap read from the rendered samples; the played sum goes through the shipped explorer's chain (both tones into one gain at −22 dB) for levels and spectra. Same start phase, same length, same chain in both arms.

The rule, set before the recorded run. The claim holds in its own situation (Δf = 2 Hz, K from 0 to 6, 60 pushes a second) if rows 1 to 5 pass: the null beats at exactly Δf; the pushed pair's beat rates fall within 1% of the per-sample pair's; both lock at the same K, on a grid of 0.01 Hz, and that K is Kc; both locks are steady; and the staircase's sidebands sit below −40 dB. Rows 6 to 10 test the Gotcha's reason and condition, and say where the claim stops. Row 7 is the control that shows the meter can see a failure: the Euler step predicts the wobble before any render. A fail in rows 6, 9 and 10 marks a place where the pushed pair stops matching the coupled one.

Whose numbers: Chromium's Web Audio (headless, the playwright build), a plain OscillatorNode and an AudioWorklet, run by this page's own code — not Tone's. Tone's frequency is a signal that ends on a native AudioParam, and setValueAtTime means the same held step there; that is from Tone's design, not from running it. The pushed arm copies the shipped explorer's tick in its order: the phases step by the last frame's gap, then the frequencies they give are pushed and held.

What the numbers can say about “feel”

“Enough to feel the locking” is a claim about a listener. No number here hears anything. What the numbers can say is narrower and still useful: in the Gotcha's own situation the pushed pair hands a listener the same cue the per-sample pair does — the same beat rate, to a thousandth of a hertz, and a lock at the same K — and adds a staircase whose traces sit about 73 dB under the tones. So whatever an ear feels with the coupled pair, it is offered the same thing by the pushed one. Where the numbers part — a slow frame rate, a big gap between the tones, a stalled frame — “feel” is not a number at all, and step 5 hands it back to you.

Listen blind

Two clips per trial, X and Y, eight seconds each: the tones start 2 Hz apart and K rises from 0 to 1.5 Hz over five seconds, so you hear drift, the slowing, and the lock. One clip is the per-sample pair, the other the pushed pair, in random order. Pick the per-sample one.

The rule, before the first trial. Four trials a round. Guessing gets each right half the time, so 4 of 4 by chance happens 1 time in 16 (6.25%). Only 4 of 4 counts as hearing a difference; anything less says nothing either way. Run the control round too: there the push rate is 20 a second and K rises to 5, where the numbers say the pushed lock shudders. If you can't pick the control, a miss on the main round doesn't tell us much. Both clips in a trial are matched in loudness over their whole length (what is left of the difference is printed), have the same start, the same length and the same 20 ms fades.
