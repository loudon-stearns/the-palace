The splice

The gotcha says a grain may jump to a random place in its waveform only where its window is shut, at the grain boundary, or every grain clicks. This walks up from one windowed grain to that claim, and measures the step each jump puts into the sound. Nothing plays until you press sound. Headphones help: the click is high and short.

Step 1 of 4 · a tone, opened and shut by a window

A grain is a short slice of tone with a window over it

Start with one grain and nothing else. A sine wave plays at 220 Hz, and a window, the Hann curve, opens it from silence and shuts it back to silence. When the window shuts, the next grain opens straight away, so you hear a tone that swells and fades at the grain rate. The read keeps its place from one grain to the next: nothing jumps yet. Watch where the window sits at each grain boundary. It is exactly zero.

TryDrag the grain length from 200 ms down to 20 ms. Long grains are a slow swell; short ones turn into a buzz at the grain rate. Either way, no clicks: nothing has jumped.

Step 2 of 4 · the scatter, at the window's zero

Each new grain starts its read somewhere random, and you can't hear the jump

Now every time the window shuts, the read jumps to a random place in the waveform. This is the scatter that gives a granular cloud its texture. Look at the faint line: the waveform breaks at every grain boundary, a hard step. Now look at the bright one, what you hear. It passes through zero there without a mark, because the window is zero at the moment of the jump, and zero times a step is nothing. The meter below measures the step each jump puts into the sound.

TrySwitch the scatter off and on. The tone changes a little, since each grain starts in a different place, but no click arrives. At this 80 ms grain the largest step reads under a millionth of full scale: the window's height at the first sample after it shuts. Shorter grains raise it, to about a hundred-thousandth at 20 ms, still far below a click.

Step 3 of 4 · the jump slid toward the middle of the grain

Move the jump off the zero, and the window lets the step through

Same grain, same scatter, one change: the slider moves where in the grain the jump lands. At the left end it lands where the window is shut, as in step 2. Slide it right and the jump lands where the window is partly open, then, at the right end, where it is wide open, in the middle of the grain. The step in the sound is the window's height there times the step in the waveform. Listen for the click arriving, once a grain.

step = w(a) · ( x(pnew) − x(pold) )
(the step you hear) is the window at the jump point a, times how far the waveform jumped. At a = 0 the window is 0 and any jump is free; at a = ½ the window is 1 and the whole jump comes through.

TryEase the slider off zero a little at a time. At 0.02 the window is 0.004; at 0.10 it is 0.095; at 0.25 it is a half. The average step follows the window, which is the whole claim in one number. Where the click first becomes audible is your ear's call, not the meter's.

Step 4 of 4 · the test

Sixteen grains at once. Does the cloud hide the click, or does every grain bring one?

A real cloud has many grains sounding together, each opening and shutting on its own beat, each jumping at its own boundary, and each a few cents off the others. Maybe so much going on buries a click. Switch between the jump at the zero and the jump mid-grain, and the meter keeps the last reading of each. The gotcha predicts a click per grain mid-grain, so the jumps a second should be the grains times the grain rate, and the top end should rise. It predicts nothing at the zero.

TrySound on, jump at the zero, wait a second; then jump mid-grain. Then crowd the cloud to 32 grains and switch again: the clicks get more frequent and each one smaller, and the top end hardly moves. Then try the bright waveform, which has a top end of its own for the clicks to sit in.
