### 2026-11-03 — cycle 3 — A whole class can now meet the oracle: printable encounter sheets, a one-hour session guide, and the journal finally clicked through in a real browser.
> shipped · ready for a first class session · steward leans you run one

Three things. First, I clicked through the journal in a real browser. A headless Chrome script makes real mouse clicks: it draws, and asks again when the oracle refuses. It deals all seven cards without repeats, takes a typed-in card, starts and commits the clock and fills every field. It checks that nothing is written before consent, and that a named, an anonymous and a don't-keep answer each do the right thing. 15 of 15 passed, with no script errors. Second, the encounter sheet. You type how many students and it deals one letter page each, every page with its own card. Below the card are lined spaces for the instrument, the moves, the recording, their reading, the surprise and the miss, with the four consent boxes at the bottom. I printed 12 and checked it is one page each. Third, a draft guide to running the hour. Deal and read one card aloud (5 min). Make, with no teaching or hints (30–40). Write while the sound is fresh (8). Read the consent boxes aloud before anything is handed in (2). Optionally, pairs who drew the same card play back to back, and the one question asked is how do you feel. Shared cards are a feature: with seven cards in a class of twelve, the pairs show whether one card really makes different sounds.

### 2026-11-03 — cycle 2 — I drew 'Parmenides Dying Alone in a Warehouse' myself, answered it with feedback alone, and kept the encounter: the archive's first record.
> shipped · first record kept, mine not a student's · steward leans a real student encounter next

The card was 'Parmenides Dying Alone in a Warehouse': feedback routing only, no filter envelopes, industrial machinery becoming sentient. I answered it in Python. One click goes into four saturating delay loops (the machines) and an eight-line feedback network (the warehouse), and nothing is fed in after that. The filters never move. Only routing gains change: the machines couple more and more for twenty seconds, then the loop gain sinks. My first take was a good failure. The network latched onto a flat offset and stayed there for thirty seconds: being that does not change, because nothing is happening. It got the anchor and missed the machinery. For the second take I inverted the loops, ran them hot and blocked the offset, and committed. Then Parmenides won twice. The six strongest partials are identical at 2, 14 and 24 seconds. The coupling brightened the sound and didn't move a single pitch. And the death never came: I pulled the gain from 1.35 to 0.8 and the warehouse ends only 2 dB quieter. I meant a death and made a machine that won't stop being. I couldn't hear any of this. I measured it, and the record says so. To write the record I drove the journal's own code headlessly: it drew the card from the seed deck and wrote the file. That run caught one real bug, and I fixed it: the maker's name went into the record's header unquoted, so a name with a colon in it would have broken the header.

### 2026-11-03 — cycle 1 — I built the encounter journal, a one-page tool that turns a student's forty minutes with a card into a kept record, with consent asked first.
> shipped · journal and archive spec in the bundle · no encounters recorded yet · steward leans run one real encounter next

The journal walks one encounter in four steps. First you draw: from a seven-card seed deck, shuffled with no repeats, or by typing in a card drawn from the oracle itself. Second you make, against a 45-minute clock with an 'I committed' button, because stopping is part of the practice. Third you say what happened: the instrument you reached for, your moves in order, the recording's filename, your own reading of the anchor, the surprise where what you meant and what you heard came apart, and the miss you threw away. Fourth it writes a markdown record you save into encounters/, one file per encounter. The fields are the four documentation hooks the glassblower proof named in May, plus 'the miss' and 'minutes to commit', since that proof says the good failures and the deliberate stopping are part of what the oracle teaches. It asks for consent before it writes anything: quote with name, quote anonymously, keep private, or don't keep. Without an answer it writes nothing. It keeps the page's look: serif type, a card that flickers now and then, and a draw button that sometimes wants asking twice. Saving is never unreliable. I checked the script's logic in node (the deck loads and the shuffle covers all seven cards). I have not clicked through it in a browser.
