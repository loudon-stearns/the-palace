Floquet · where the ladder comes from

The modulator’s Fourier series, and what a loop does to it

The page derives the sideband ladder by multiplying a carrier by a periodic modulation, and says the modulation’s Fourier series is the spectral envelope. Three steps: ring modulation, the simplest case; four modulator shapes, each laid against its own coefficients; and then the test, the same modulators put inside a resonating loop, where the page’s Stage 1 instrument lives.

1 · Ring modulation

Multiply a 220 Hz tone by an 80 Hz cosine. The 220 Hz is gone; in its place are two tones, at 220 − 80 and 220 + 80. That is the whole of ring modulation, and the simplest system whose coefficient changes in time.

cos(2π·220t) · cos(2π·80t) = ½ cos(2π·140t) + ½ cos(2π·300t)
(the tone) times (the modulator) = half a tone at the difference + half a tone at the sum

2 · Shape the modulator

Any periodic modulator is a sum of cosines at 80, 160, 240 Hz and so on, with weights cn. Each one makes its own pair of sidebands, so the rung at 220 + n·80 Hz should stand exactly as tall as |cn|. Pick a shape: the amber stems are measured off the sound, the blue rings are the modulator’s own coefficients.

3 · Inside the loop

The test. Stage 1 is not a multiplier; it is a resonator whose stiffness is pumped, so every sideband goes round the loop and meets the modulator again. Here a resonator tuned to 220 Hz (ζ = 0.05) is driven at 220 Hz while the same modulator pumps it by depth h. Amber is measured, relative to the carrier; blue is the first-order prediction, each cn weighed by the resonator’s own response at that rung.

Each setting is computed afresh: half a second to settle, then exactly one second read. Levels are dB relative to the 220 Hz line. A rung reads “none” below −120 dB.
