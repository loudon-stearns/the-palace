A string and a drum head — who decides the partials?

Two struck things at the same pitch, 140 Hz. A string, whose modes are sines, and an ideal round drum head, whose modes are Bessel functions. Each mode sounds at the height it has where you strike. The left panel is the instrument and the strike point; the right is where its partials fall, as multiples of the lowest one, with the whole numbers ruled in. Walk from the string to the drum; the last step lets you strike the drum anywhere.

A string's n-th mode is a sine with n half-waves along it, and it sounds at n times the lowest: whole numbers, because a line's boundary is two points.
um,k(r, θ) = Jm( jm,k · r ) · cos( mθ )  ·  fm,k = f0,1 · jm,k ⁄ j0,1
A round head's mode has m lines across it and k rings; its shape is the m-th Bessel function, and the rim, where it must be still, can only sit on a zero of it, jm,k. So it sounds at the ratio of that zero to the first one: 2.4048, 3.8317, 5.1356, 5.5201 … give 1, 1.594, 2.136, 2.295 …
An ideal membrane in a vacuum: no air, no stiffness, no shell. A real kettle drum's air pulls its (1,1), (2,1) and (3,1) modes toward 2 : 3 : 4, which is how a timpani gets a pitch. Each mode's height is its own shape at the strike point, softened by one mallet, the same for both; how it radiates is left out. One decay law serves both instruments, so only where the partials fall differs. A mode with lines across it comes as a pair at one frequency and turns to face the strike, so only the strike's distance from the centre matters; the test searches 99 of them, centre to rim.
