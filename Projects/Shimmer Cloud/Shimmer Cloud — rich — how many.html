How many copies before it stops sounding like a chorus?

Open Questions asks how many pitch-shifted copies the listener needs before they hear shimmer rather than chorus. Every take here is May's droplet cloud: the phrase copied many times, each copy played a few cents fast or slow, started somewhere in the first 300 ms and panned somewhere left to right. We start with one voice and end on a square that sets the number of copies against how far apart they are tuned.

One voice

The phrase, dry: Kokoro reading "the prism, and the cloud." Its pitch sits near 200 Hz (measured: 202 Hz median). Every cloud on the next steps is made only of copies of this.

More copies

Now copies, all tuned within about 5 cents of the voice. May's set starts at 30; the first four rungs, 2 to 12, were rendered for this piece with her engine and seed, because the line this question asks about may sit down there. Listen to how the swirl changes: two or three copies beat in a few regular pulses you could count, the chorus of a pedal; by about a dozen the pulses blur into a random swirl, and more copies leave it as it is. The regularity readout measures that. Count does one more thing: each copy starts at its own moment in the first 300 ms, so it is also how many onsets arrive a second. Schroeder (1962) found about a thousand a second makes a flutter-free reverb, and that the ear can't tell a thousand from any higher number even on short transients. So 300, 800 and 2500 copies should not differ in grain: a prediction your ear can check.

onsets per second = N ⁄ T
(how many copies begin each second) = (the number of copies) ÷ (the window they start in, here 300 ms)

Wider spread

Hold the count at 400 copies and widen how far apart they are tuned, from about 1 cent to about 75. Two copies a few cents apart beat: their sum gets louder and softer as they drift in and out of step. A crowd of copies does the same, all at once, and how fast the level swirls depends on how far apart they are tuned. The swirl is measured on May's engine with a steady 200 Hz tone in place of the phrase, since the phrase's own pitch moves too much to measure it.

swirl rate ≈ f · (ln 2 ⁄ 1200) · (2 ⁄ √π) · σ
(how often the crowd's level rises and falls) ≈ (the pitch) × (how far one cent moves a pitch: 0.058 %) × 1.13 × (the spread in cents). At 200 Hz and 5 cents: 0.65 times a second.

One cent is close to step 2's tight tuning: the same crowd, its swirl slowed to almost nothing.

The same steady-tone measurement across every take on these steps. The swirl's rate is set by spread (at 400 copies, 1 to 75 cents moves it from 0.23 to 9.4 times a second) and barely by count. Its depth sits near 0.5 everywhere. What count changes is the swirl's kind, and only at the bottom: at 25 cents its strongest lines carry 0.96 of its power with 2 copies, 0.88 with 3, 0.40 with 6, then 0.20 to 0.25 from 12 copies up. At 5 cents the swirl is so slow that ten seconds hold too few of its cycles to read below about 0.5. The 1.13 is copy against copy: these takes carry no dry voice; with the dry mixed in, copy against dry would bring 0.80. A rate-resampled copy is a Doppler shift, so the optical twin of this crowd is light scattered off moving particles, the flicker called speckle. The question asked again for lenses belongs to the live cloud the project's plan builds next, with count, and spread beside it, on controls.
