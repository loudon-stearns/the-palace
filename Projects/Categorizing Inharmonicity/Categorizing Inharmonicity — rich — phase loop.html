Phase only, in a loop

§ 6 says a chain of all-pass filters changes no level, only phase, and still makes a tone inharmonic. We start with a loop that only delays and end on that claim, measured on the sound itself. Every spectrum here is read from the rendered audio, not drawn from a formula. Silent until you press a button.

A loop that only delays

Pluck a loop: a short pulse goes in, comes round after D samples a little quieter, and goes round again. It rings only where a whole number of cycles fits the trip, so its partials land on whole-number multiples of one frequency: the harmonic series, from nothing but delay. This is the dry tone everything after is measured against.

ω · D = 2πk   →   fk = k · 48000 ⁄ D
(the loop rings where its phase over one trip) = (a whole number of turns), so (the kth partial) = k × (the sample rate ÷ the delay in samples). D = 218 gives 220.18 Hz.

The all-passes outside the loop

Now send that dry tone once through a chain of all-pass filters, the way § 6's synthesis list says: feed a harmonic tone through the cascade. An all-pass passes every frequency at full level and only delays it, by an amount that depends on the frequency. Watch the wave change shape. Then read where the partials are.

H(z) = (a + z⁻¹) ⁄ (1 + a · z⁻¹),   |H| = 1
(one all-pass) = (the coefficient plus a one-sample delay) ÷ (one plus the coefficient times a one-sample delay); its gain is exactly one at every frequency.

a = 0 makes each all-pass one plain sample of delay: the dry tone, a few samples late.

The all-passes inside the loop

Put the same chain inside the loop, and shorten the plain delay so partial 1 stays near 220 Hz. Now every trip round adds the chain's phase again, and the frequencies the loop rings at are wherever the delay's phase and the chain's phase add to a whole number of turns. With a negative coefficient the chain delays high frequencies less, so they come round sooner: the upper partials run sharp. A positive coefficient pulls them flat, but only by a cent or so here: its delay changes mostly far above these partials.

ω · D + M · φ(ω) = 2πk,   φ(ω) = ω − 2 · atan( a · sin ω ⁄ (1 + a · cos ω) )
(the delay's phase) + (M all-passes' phase lag) = a whole number of turns, where (one all-pass's lag) = (one sample's worth) minus twice the angle set by the coefficient. Small ticks mark where this puts each partial; the measured peaks should sit on them.

a = 0 gives step 1 again: every partial back on its ghost.

Is it all phase?

The section's claim: inharmonicity can be entirely phase-based, with no amplitude modulation. Here is everything measured at once, with a control: the same loop, but its chain swapped for plain delay of the same length at low frequencies, phase in a straight line. If the claim holds, the gain is flat, the partials move, and the straight-line control does not move them.

This is the trick a digital piano string uses for its stiffness: a cascade of first-order all-passes in the string's loop (Van Duyne and Smith, 1994). A fixed chain gives a fixed tone; nothing here evolves unless the coefficient moves.
