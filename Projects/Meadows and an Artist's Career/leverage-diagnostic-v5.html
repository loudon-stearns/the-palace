Write down your last ten career decisions — and see where they sit

Donella Meadows ranked twelve places to push on a system, from weakest (tweak a number) to strongest (let go of what the whole thing is for). Write each of your last ten real career decisions in a sentence, then put it on the rung it belongs to. This is closer to a journal than a quiz: the sentences you write are the point, and the shape is what they add up to. Under every rung you'll find the moves Brian Eno, Pauline Oliveros and Agnes Martin made there, so you can recognise your own by holding it against a real one. The bar at the top keeps your shape next to theirs while you work. When all ten are placed, the exercise at the bottom takes your lowest decision one move up the ladder.

Write your ten first, a sentence each, in the box below: what you decided and what it changed. Then read down the rungs and file each one where it fits. Every rung opens (sit with this rung) into a few lines on what Meadows meant by it and a question to write about. You don't have to answer them all. Answer the ones that catch. One warning before you compare: biographies remember the high rungs and forget the afternoons, while your memory does the opposite. If your shape sits lower than theirs, part of that gap is real and part is the difference between a biography and a calendar.

● First · in your own words

Your last ten decisions

Go back a few months. A decision counts if you acted on it: money moved, a calendar changed, a thing shipped, a no got said. Write each one plainly, the way you'd tell a friend: what you decided, and what it changed (or didn't). Don't sort them yet. Writing them before you see the ladder keeps you honest about which ones you'd rather forget.

● Then · looking at the shape

What the shape tells you

The reading above is a machine's guess. Yours counts more. Two questions, and room to write.

Which of your ten did you file lower than you expected, or higher? What does that tell you about where your attention goes?

How do you feel, having looked at it?

Not what you think of it — how it sits in the body. Relief, a sinking, a quiet "I knew that", an itch to change something. That feeling is information the numbers don't carry.
