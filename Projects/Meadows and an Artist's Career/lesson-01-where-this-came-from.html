Where the ladder came from

A ladder for deciding where to push in your own working life started as a week of posts about a kick drum. This lesson tells that story, lets you play with one loop that lives both in a mix and in a career, and ends with three things to write.

about 20 minutes · one loop to play with · three journal prompts · nothing leaves this browser

1A kick drum, and then thirty-two exercises

In March 2026 Loudon was planning a week of social posts called The Kick Drum Paradox: small decisions, infinite implications. The idea was Donella Meadows' 1999 essay Leverage Points: Places to Intervene in a System. Meadows was a systems scientist. She listed twelve places to push on any system, from the weakest (change a number) to the strongest (change the way of seeing that the whole system grew out of).

Partway through, the plan stopped being a plan:

I found many more connections between her essay and music production. I am curious if you can find more connections…Loudon, March 2026

Thirty-two exercises came out of that, across composition, production and wellbeing. Then came the honest part. Loudon rated every one of them on four questions: does it ring true, would I teach it, is it deep, is it how I actually work. Eleven survived.

The ones that scored highest weren't about mixing. They were a weekly review, a deadline told to three friends, a rule that frees you by closing a door, journaling. Loudon noticed it while it was happening:

I feel these questions following my personal journey from musician teacher to life coach, is it possible for me to make that shift?Loudon, March 2026

That's where this project comes from. The ladder that worked on a mix worked better on the life that makes the mix. This series points it there.

2The first lesson was a question

The exercise that opened the eleven is a koan, not a rule. Hold a drone and change the bass note under everything. What kind of change was that? Meadows would call the note a number, the weakest kind of push. But change it and the whole piece moves. Loudon put it like this:

Changing the bass note is the most powerful of a parameter that there is… But even changing the bass note has a limited impact compared to other changes, like changing the rules of the music.Loudon, on the Drone Koan

Keep that in your pocket for the whole series. The ladder is useful, and it is also too neat. Make the change first, feel what it did, and only then ask which rung it was on. A decision can sit on two rungs, and the honest answer is sometimes "I'm not sure."

3One loop, two places

Some connections between a mix and a career are only metaphors. This one is the same loop. A loop that keeps something near a target — a compressor holding a level, an artist checking whether the work is landing — only works as well as its response time. Loudon's note on the sidechain compressor's attack:

If that is too high (slow response) the benefits go away, but if too fast we get unwanted artifacts.Loudon, on the sidechain exercise

Meadows' own picture is a hotel shower. You turn the tap, the hot water takes a few seconds to reach you, so you turn it further, and then you're scalded. In a career the delay is the time between making something and hearing back. Play with it:

a loop chasing a target

in the mixThe target is the level you want. The response time is the attack. Slow, and the kick has passed before the compressor moves.
in the careerThe target is where you want the work to be. The response time is weeks between finishing something and anyone hearing it.

Try it with a long wait and a hard correction. The line swings past the target and back, wider each time. That's an artist who releases once every few years and then changes everything at once. Now cut the wait to one week and correct gently. It settles. Shortening the wait is rung 9 on the ladder, and for most working artists it's the cheapest rung there is to move.

5Write

Not a quiz. Write a few sentences for each, or a page. It stays in this browser, and you can save it as a text file.

What's your drone?

The one thing under everything you make that you've never thought of changing. An instrument, a city, a day job, a genre, a sentence about who you are. Is it a number or a rule?

Where do you wait longest before you hear back?

Name the thing, and roughly how many weeks. Then name one way that wait could be cut in half.

How do you feel?

After reading and writing this. Only you can answer it, and there's no wrong answer.
