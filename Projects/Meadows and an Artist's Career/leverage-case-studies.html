What the three shapes teach

Each of them has a home rung, and it is not the same one. Eno lives at the paradigm — he keeps redefining what a record is — and comes down to the knobs only to serve that. Oliveros builds structures that let a practice reproduce itself: a scene, a school, a foundation, a certificate. Martin is the strangest: she fixed her lowest rung once — one square format, one motif — and then never spent attention there again, which freed everything for rules and goals.
Eno's level-12 move is the lesson in miniature. Choosing tape-loop lengths for Music for Airports is as low as leverage gets — a number. But it was set inside a level-2 move (music as a place, not a performance), so the number carried the paradigm. A parameter isn't low-leverage because it's small; it's low-leverage when nothing above it is pointing it anywhere.
Read these shapes with suspicion — biographies record the high rungs. None of these three come out inverted, and that is partly an artifact: nobody writes down the afternoon Eno spent on a synth patch or the month Martin spent on a gallery invoice. Your own memory works the other way — it keeps the small, recent, tangible decisions and loses the slow ones. So when your shape looks heavier at the bottom than theirs, some of that gap is real and some is the difference between a biography and a calendar. The honest comparison is your ten against your ten from five years ago. Look at rung 9, too: it is empty for all three. No biography records how long an idea took to ship — yet cycle time may be the rung a working artist can move most cheaply this month.
The rung that pays most arrives with a cost. Oliveros gave up a tenured professorship — a buffer, level 11 — to make her level-4 move. Martin gave up painting itself, at the height of her recognition. Neither move was cheap, and the worksheet's own note holds here: a paradigm move without runway is how good careers stall halfway through changing.
