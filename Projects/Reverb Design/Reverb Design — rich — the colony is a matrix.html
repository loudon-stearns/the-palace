The colony is a matrix

On the left, the dish. On the right, the drawing a reverb engineer would make of it: delay lines in a row, and a square of numbers that says how much of each line's sound is fed back into every other. They look like different machines. Knock on one, then the other, then on one minus the other.

The newborn dish: one spore, three short threads, three dead ends. Fold each thread at its tip and it is one delay line as long as the round trip. The spore is where the three lines meet, and what it does to them is the square of numbers: a third of each line comes back down itself upside down, two thirds of it goes down each of the others. Three delay lines and a matrix is a feedback delay network. The dish was one before it grew a single thread.

Give the spore more threads and make them longer, each a different length so their echoes do not land on the same instants. The square grows a row and a column for each. This shape, a ring of delay lines fed back through one matrix that loses nothing, is the feedback delay network of the reverb papers, and this particular matrix, the same number everywhere except down the diagonal, is the one Jot proposed. Rocchesso and Smith showed in 1997 that such a network and a star of waveguides are the same thing. Here you can hear that they are.

Now let the spore grow. Every thread is two delay lines, one each way, so the row of lines gets long. The matrix gets large and almost empty: sorted so that the lines arriving at one place sit together, it is a string of small squares down the diagonal, one for every fork, each the same kind of square as the spore's, and a lone dot for every dead end. The threads do the rest: each carries what leaves one square to another. A textbook network has every line talking to every other. A colony's lines talk only to their neighbours. That is the whole difference, and it is why the colony takes a fifth of a second to blur.

The diagram's network is written in this piece from the colony's wiring alone: which thread meets which, how long, how thick. It never calls the dish's sound code. Both are rendered before they play, and the difference is played at the same volume as the other two, so if you hear nothing, that is the result. Here the colony grows on plucked strings fed in silence when you press; the dish at the top of the page is where it grows on what you play it.
