The knee

The section says the Disorder macro drives a real phase transition, not a crossfade: the flock snaps from noise into a tone. This is the page's own flock, line for line, run here and heard through its own grain voice, walked up from one bird to that claim. Nothing plays until you press sound.

Step 1 of 4 · one bird and its neighbours

A bird turns toward the way its neighbours fly, and noise knocks it off

Start with one bird and one rule. Its neighbours, the grey darts, fly one way. Every frame the bird steers a little toward their average heading, never more than a small fixed nudge, and every frame the Disorder knob twists its heading by a random angle. Here a heading is a pitch, one octave around the circle, so you hear it: the low steady tone is the neighbours, the other is the bird. With no disorder the bird settles onto their note. Turn disorder up and it can't hold it.

Step 2 of 4 · sixty birds, one order parameter

Every bird does the same, and the flock gets one number: φ

Now sixty birds, each one the bird from step 1, each the others' neighbour, with separation and cohesion as on the instrument. Nobody holds the whole flock's heading, yet one can appear. φ measures it: add every bird's heading as an arrow of length one and see how long the sum is, per bird. All one way, φ is 1. Every bird its own way, φ sits near a floor set only by how many birds there are, about 0.11 for sixty. The arrow at the left of the plot is that sum. The cloud you hear is the instrument's own: heading is pitch, and a bird held on course plays long tonal grains.

Step 3 of 4 · φ against the slider

Sweep the knob and draw what the flock does

A crossfade does what the knob says: halfway along, halfway between. The dashed line is that, from the ordered flock at one end to the floor at the other. The bright curve is what this flock does, each point settled for forty seconds and averaged over eight starts (the steward's sweep). The flock holds its order, then gives it up over a short stretch, then stays melted: a knee. Sweep the live flock and its dots land near the curve, trailing behind it, because a moving flock has no time to settle.

Step 4 of 4 · the test

A knee alone proves nothing. A phase transition leaves three more marks.

Any knob can be bent into a knee: square its travel and it bends. What only a real transition does is this. Its wobble peaks at the knee, because there the flock can't decide. Its knee gets sharper as the flock gets bigger, at the same crowding, heading for a true corner in a flock without end. And a continuous ("second-order") transition comes back the same way it went, with no memory of which side it came from. Each curve below is this page's flock run at a size, every point settled thirty seconds, then measured twenty, over six starts; the field grows with the flock so each bird has the same room.
