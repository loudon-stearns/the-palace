Bend it or fret it

One plain steel guitar string, the G, 0.43 mm thick on a 648 mm scale, tuned to 196 Hz. Two ways to raise its pitch: pull it tighter, or make it shorter. The section says the first one changes the timbre. Silent until you press a button.

One string at rest

Everything about the string goes into one number. Its steel, its thickness, the tension that tunes it to G and its length give B, and B sets how far each partial runs sharp. The bars show that, partial by partial, in cents above n times the pitch you hear.

B = π³ · E · d⁴ / (64 · T · L²)
(the inharmonicity coefficient) = π cubed × (the steel's stiffness) × (the diameter to the fourth power) ÷ (64 × the tension × the length squared).

Bend it

Push the string across the fret and it stretches: the tension rises by a factor k. Pitch goes as the square root of tension, and B goes as one over it, so the bars shrink as the note rises. The string is the same steel and the same length; only its tension changed.

f₀ → √k · f₀     B → B / k
(the fundamental) rises by the square root of (the tension multiplier); (the coefficient) falls by the multiplier itself.

A bend of 0 cents is step 1.

Fret it instead

The other way up: press the string at a fret, so the part that rings is shorter by a factor ρ. Pitch rises by ρ and the tension stays put, but B goes as one over the length squared, so the bars grow. The same pitch, reached this way, comes with more stretch, not less.

f₀ → ρ · f₀     B → B · ρ²
(the fundamental) rises by (how many times shorter the string is); (the coefficient) rises by that factor squared.

0 cents is the open string of step 1; 100, 200 and 300 are the first three frets.

Same pitch, two ways

The section's claim: a bent note sounds subtly different from an unbent note at the same pitch, because the bent string has lower inharmonicity. On a guitar the unbent note at that pitch is the fretted one. Here both reach exactly the same first partial, decay alike, and differ only in B. A third string gets there by turning its tuning peg instead, so its tension rises just as the bend's does.

Measured on the formula, not by ear: an agent can't hear whether a few cents at the tenth partial is audible, and the page's own open questions ask where that threshold sits. The stiffer string is not a guitar; it is there to find what to listen for. Then switch it off and listen again. The bend here starts from the open string, where a real bend usually starts from a fretted note; the ratio is the same from any fret, because bending from fret m and fretting at m + 2 both start from the same B.
