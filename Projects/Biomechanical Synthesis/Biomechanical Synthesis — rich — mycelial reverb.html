A reverb you grow

There is a spore in the dish. Knock on it. The sound runs out along the threads, and wherever it reaches a growing tip, the tip grows. Keep feeding it and the dry little knock you started with comes back from a room that was not there a minute ago.

The colony is made of three small things, and each numbered button puts one of them in the dish alone to knock on. As it stands: sound goes in at the amber ring and comes out at the fruiting bodies, each one placed left to right as it sits in the dish. Until the first one appears you are listening at the spore. A tip grows only while sound is reaching it, so a colony fed in one corner grows from that corner. A frost kills every dead end back by one thread; what survives is whatever had joined up into a ring.

y(t) = x(t) + r · g² · y(t − 2L)

What you hear now is the knock, plus what you heard one round trip ago (2L: down the thread and back), made smaller by the end it turned at (r, here 0.8: each end keeps a fifth) and by the way there and back (g).

One hypha, two ends. A pulse runs down it, turns round, comes back: an echo. Now shorten the thread. The echoes crowd together until you cannot count them, and somewhere under about 25 ms you stop hearing a rhythm and start hearing a note. Same thread, same arithmetic.

outi = 2√yi · ( Σ √yj · inj ) / Σ yj − ini

What leaves a junction down thread i is the junction's shared pressure (everything arriving, each thread weighted by its thickness y) less what arrived on thread i itself. For three equal threads that is: a third comes back upside down, and two thirds goes on down each of the other two.

A branch. The pulse reaches the fork and has to be shared out. Nothing is made there and nothing is lost: add up the squares of what leaves and you get the squares of what arrived, for any number of threads of any thickness. That one fact is why the whole colony cannot run away, however it grows.

loops = threads − joins + 1

Count the threads, take away the places they meet or end, add one. A tree gives zero: every way out is a dead end. Each fusion adds one way round.

A fusion. When a growing hypha meets another it can fuse with it, and now there is a way round. The pulse goes round both ways at once and meets itself coming back, so the echoes stop being evenly spaced. Dead ends eat sound here (each tip keeps a fifth); a ring has no ends, so a ring holds on to it.

Put those together and let it grow. These are impulse responses of the engine in this page, rendered without a browser, on twelve colonies fed the same plucked strings (medians; carry 3 s, soil 0.25).

Fed nothing but the same strings, the tail went from 0.40 s at three threads to 2.22 s with the dish full: longer in 12 of 12 colonies. A frost, pressed until nothing more died, took every dead end (17 to 0) and left 117 threads, all of them in rings. That rang for 2.66 s: longer in 12 of 12. Dead ends eat sound and rings keep it. One guess, written down before the first run, is not settled. It said the echoes would blur into a wash sooner as the colony grew. Three threads never make a wash and ten do, about 215 ms in. From ten threads to a full dish it came sooner in 10 colonies and later in 2, but the median moved only from 215 to 200 ms, and an earlier tuning of the growth rules gave 5 sooner and 7 later. So growing lengthens the tail, the wash arrives with the first few branches, and past that this piece claims nothing.

Can it blow up? With nothing going in, the energy held in the network was read every 64 samples over 160 runs, at the corners of every control and past what these sliders reach (carry out to 12 s, no damping, tips that give everything back). The largest step was 1.000000 of the one before, where anything over 1 would be a rise. Then the torture: a minute of full-scale noise while the colony grew, was frosted, and every control jumped four times a second. The loudest sample out was 1.51 times the input, every sample a finite number, and 100 dB gone twenty seconds after the noise stopped. Failure would have been one rise, or one sample that was not a number. The page puts a compressor after the dish as well, for your ears.

What is the fungus, and what is the instrument

The instrument borrows how a mycelium is built. It does not model sound moving through one: I looked for a measurement of that and found none. Here is which is which.

measured, in fungi

Hyphae lengthen at the tip, branch, and fuse with one another, and that is how a colony becomes a network and not a tree.

Fricker, Heaton, Jones & Boddy, “The Mycelium as a Network”, Microbiology Spectrum, 2017. orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/102028

in the instrument

Tips extend, branch and fuse by simple rules with a seeded dice. The shapes look fungal. The angles, lengths and odds are mine, fitted to nothing.

measured, in fungi

The woodland fungus Phanerochaete velutina thickens the routes it uses and recycles the rest, and its network gets better at carrying and harder to cut as it grows.

Bebber, Hynes, Darrah, Boddy & Fricker, Proc. R. Soc. B 274:2307–2315, 2007. pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2288531

in the instrument

A thread that carries more of the sound thickens and takes a larger share at each junction. The frost stands in for recycling: it is a button, and a fungus has no such season switch.

measured, in fungi

Growth draws material through the network toward the tips: water taken up behind the front has to go somewhere, and it goes to where the colony is getting bigger.

Heaton, López, Maini, Fricker & Jones, “Growth-induced mass flows in fungal networks”, Proc. R. Soc. B 277:3265–3274, 2010. arxiv.org/abs/1005.5305

in the instrument

A tip keeps a fifth of every pulse that reaches it and grows on what it gets. That sound is food is the metaphor the whole piece stands on. It is not a finding.

measured, once

One lab study played an 8 kHz tone at 80 dB to Trichoderma harzianum for five days and found more biomass and more spore activity than in silence. One fungus, one tone, dishes.

Robinson, Annells, Cando-Dumancela & Breed, Biology Letters 20:20240295, 2024. pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11444772

in the instrument

Growth here follows the sound thread by thread, within seconds. Nothing in that study says a tip grows toward a sound. Don't read the dish as a model of it.

measured, and argued over

Electrical pulses have been recorded in hyphae since 1995. A 2022 paper read spikes lasting 1 to 21 hours as something like words; a 2024 reply says the recordings may be artefacts and are no language.

Olsson & Hansson, Naturwissenschaften 82:30–31, 1995. Adamatzky, R. Soc. Open Sci. 9:211926, 2022, arxiv.org/abs/2112.09907. Blatt et al., Fungal Ecology 68:101326, 2024.

in the instrument

A pulse crosses this dish in a third of a second. Whatever the hours-long signals in a real fungus are, the glow you see here is audio, not that.

measured, in fungi

Fruiting is set off by conditions: less nitrogen and carbon to eat, a drop in temperature, and in some species light.

Sakamoto, Fungal Biology Reviews 32(4):236, 2018. doi:10.1016/j.fbr.2018.02.003

in the instrument

A fruiting body comes up at the busiest junction that has room. That is a rule for where to listen, not how a fungus decides.

measured, in physics

Delay lines joined at junctions that share out what arrives are a digital waveguide network, the standard way to model strings and tubes, and a known kind of reverb.

Smith, “Physical Audio Signal Processing”, online book, CCRMA, Stanford. ccrma.stanford.edu/~jos/pasp
