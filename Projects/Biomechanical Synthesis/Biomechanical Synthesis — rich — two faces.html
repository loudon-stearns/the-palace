Does the body change what your hand sets?

The page says you will never forget how a compressor works after pumping blood through arteries. That is a claim about memory, over weeks, in students; no sitting with a web page can test it, and this one doesn't try. What a page can test is smaller: build one compressor, give it two faces, a plain meter and a body, prove they are the same arithmetic, and then ask whether the face changes what a hand sets on the same task. Five steps, one thing in each. Nothing plays until you press Play.

the null · plain output minus body output, on the current settings · a check of the code
The body face is written in its own variables, pressure in mmHg and a lumen in millimetres, not by calling the plain one. This checks the code, not the world: the body face is the same smoother rescaled into mmHg and millimetres, so if it was coded as the same compressor, subtracting one output from the other leaves nothing: the residual must be below −120 dB against the signal. Anything above that would mean the faces are two compressors. The control is a body whose wall moves in millimetres instead of in ratios of its width, the way a vessel drawn on screen most naturally moves; the null must fail it (above −60 dB on an engaged setting), or the null could not tell two compressors apart.

the test · three tries, one task
The task, the same each time: bring the kicks down so their deepest squeeze sits in the band (4 to 8 dB; a light says when it does), without the hiss between hits swelling and sinking. Set it so it sounds right to you, then press Done. Three tries; every try starts from the same settings, and the numbers stay hidden until the end. What is being compared is shown after the third try, not before, so that knowing it can’t steer your hand.
The rule, fixed in this piece’s code before anyone’s first try, shown now that you are done.
  1. A coin you were not shown picked the order: plain, body, plain, or body, plain, body. Both faces had the same ranges, order, slider travel and start; only the words and the picture differ, and the sound is one compressor on one loop.
  2. Within you. Your three settings become points (threshold straight; ratio, attack and release on a log scale; each range 0..1). If your two tries on the same face are the closest pair, the face moved your settings on this run. If the face changes nothing, any pair is equally likely to be closest: chance 1 in 3. Pooled, the face moves what readers set if the count reaches 4 of 5, 7 of 10, 11 of 20 or 19 of 40 runs (each p ≤ 0.05 against 1 in 3), with the runs whose repeated face was plain and those whose was the body counted apart, each held to the same table. This half can be gamed by a reader who knows the rule before trying, which is why it was hidden.
  3. Between readers, first tries only. On try 1 nobody has seen the other face. Readers whose coin gave the body first are compared with readers whose coin gave the plain face first, on log attack and log release: difference of the two groups’ means, two-sided permutation p over 9999 shuffles. The face moved first tries if either p is under 0.025 (two measures, so 5% overall by chance). It needs 5 readers in each arm; both arms’ counts and medians are always shown. It is a small net: simulated, with 10 readers an arm it finds a body face that quadruples attack and release about half the time, with 20 an arm most of the time.
  4. What this cannot rule on: memory, which is the page’s claim, and disgust, which this face doesn’t try for. Learning across tries pulls try 1 and try 3 apart, which works against the face in the first half; the second half uses first tries only.
