What is harmony?

The first twenty minutes of the Inharmonic Wavetable Synthesis lesson. Six stops. Sound on — every stop plays.

1 · A flat line

Across the box runs partial number: the 1st partial at the left, the 24th at the right. Up and down is how far each partial sits from its harmonic place, in cents. Every partial on the line sits at exactly 1, 2, 3, 4… times the fundamental. That flat line is the harmonic series. It is the whole answer: harmony is a flat line.

24 partials, 220 Hz, each one 1/n as loud — a soft saw.

Listen for one thing: it sounds like one note, not twenty-four. That fusing is what a flat line buys you.

2 · Bend it

Now drag on the box, or move the slider. Stiffness bends the line upward: each partial goes a little sharp, and the higher ones go sharper. This is what happens in a real string, because a stiff wire resists bending most at its short, high wiggles.

fₙ = n · f₀ · √(1 + B·n²) — partial_freq = number · fundamental · √(1 + stiffness · number²)

Start small and creep up. Somewhere the note stops being one note. Where did it happen for you?

3 · A real piano

That bend is not made up. These dots were measured from real piano recordings: each partial's actual pitch, in cents off its harmonic place. The thin curve is the stiffness formula fitted through them. A low string barely bends. A short treble string bows hard.

Both are played at the same pitch here, so the only difference you hear is the bend. The treble curve on a low note is a string no piano has — and it sounds like metal.

4 · A bell

A bell does not bend the line. It throws its partials off it. These are the measured modes of a small struck bell, and they are nowhere near 1, 2, 3, 4. To put a bell on the line you have to decide which slot each mode belongs to. There are two honest answers, and the instrument keeps both.

Rank: the bell's 1st mode goes in slot 1, its 2nd in slot 2, and so on. Nearest: each mode goes in the harmonic slot closest to its real pitch, and the louder one wins when two want the same slot.

At full bend the two filings are nearly the same bell; Nearest only drops the quieter of two modes that want one slot. Now pull the bend to zero under each. Rank flattens the bell into an organ. Nearest flattens it onto the few harmonics it was hiding near. Same bell, two different harmonies underneath it.

5 · The line decides the scale

Play two notes together. Where their partials nearly line up, they rub and beat; where they line up exactly, the rubbing stops. The curve below adds up all that rubbing for every interval from unison to a little past an octave (William Sethares' method). Its valleys are the intervals that sound smooth for this sound.

or click anywhere on the curve to hear that interval

With a flat line the valleys sit on the familiar intervals and the octave is exactly 1200 cents. Bend the line and watch the octave valley slide sharp: a few cents at real piano stiffness (the first fifth of the slider), sixty cents at the far end, where the string has become a bar. The piano tuner's stretched octave is this valley, found by ear. The sound decides which notes agree.

6 · Your turn

You have heard harmony as a flat line, bent it by hand, heard two real strings, filed a bell two ways and watched the scale slide. One question, and only you can answer it.

How do you feel? And: if you could draw any line on that box, what material would you want it to be?
