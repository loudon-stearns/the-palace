Two walls, one line

A real organ wavetable decides how loud each partial is. A physical trajectory decides where each partial sits. The line below is the instrument's whole state: flat means harmony, every bend is a material, and its glow is the organ.

Strike: τB runs from attack to rest on its own clock while upper partials die first. Hold: you drive τB. Keys a s d f play the four notes, g the chord. Start with the chord on "bell", Strike, settle 4 s.

the two walls of the cube

the engine, twice

fₙ = n · f₀ · 2^(B · Δcₙ(τB) / 1200)
(frequency of partial n) = (partial number) × (fundamental) × 2^((B depth) × (cents off harmonic at the B frame) / 1200)
out = Σₙ Aₙ(τA) · envₙ · sin(φₙ)
(sound) = sum over partials of (organ level at the A frame) × (envelope) × sin(running phase)

The two clocks are the point. τA moves the brightness, τB moves the bend, and neither knows about the other. Play the chord on the bell and listen to the interval: while the partials sit on membrane positions, the upper partials of the three notes stop lining up, so a chord that should sit still beats and smears; as the line goes flat they line up again and it clears. The scale you can use depends on where the line is. That is Sethares, played rather than read.
