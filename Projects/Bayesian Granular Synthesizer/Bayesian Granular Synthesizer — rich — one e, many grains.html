Bayesian Granular Synthesizer · the statistics underneath

One e, many grains

The page names four pieces of statistics under the cloud: Poisson onsets, the exponential, the Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem. Each one can be counted in the cloud itself. Four steps, and the last is a test.

1 · Grains arrive at random

The cloud starts grains at random moments, at a steady average rate: a Poisson process. Nothing remembers when the last grain came, so short waits are the most common and long ones get rarer by the same factor for every extra millisecond. The gaps between onsets fall away as an exponential curve.

The counts come from the engine's own onsets, twenty seconds at the rate you set, nothing held. The KS distance is the largest gap between the counted and the predicted share of gaps up to any length; below its 5% line the counts give no reason to doubt the curve. A fair test still crosses that line at about one setting in twenty. Onsets land on whole samples, so the gaps come in steps of 1 ⁄ 48,000 s; twenty seconds of grains is too few to see the steps.

2 · The same e, twice

The evidence envelopes are exponentials too: a held note's weight rises as 1 − e^(−t ⁄ 80 ms), and a released note's weight falls as e^(−t ⁄ 1.4 s). Measure each in its own unit, the gaps in mean gaps and the envelopes in their time constants, and they are one curve. Same e, two different things: one is a probability, how long you wait for the next grain; the other is a decay, how fast a let-go note is forgotten.

The envelopes are the engine's own, stepped every 64 samples as it runs. The waiting is counted from step 1's twenty seconds, at its rate. The envelopes match e^(−x) to rounding because they are made of it; the waiting matches because a Poisson process forces it, and only as closely as twenty seconds of grains can show.

3 · Many draws fill out the belief

Hold C3, E4 and G4 until the cloud is sure, then count where the grains land, semitone by semitone. A few grains say little. Many grains trace the belief itself: the share in each semitone settles on what the belief gives it. That is the Law of Large Numbers. Watch the average pitch too: it settles on one pitch, and it is not a note of the chord.

"Distance from the belief" is half the summed difference between counted and predicted shares, over every semitone: 0 is a perfect match, 1 is no overlap. It shrinks about as 1 ⁄ √N.

4 · The test: many grains summed into a bell

The page says the Central Limit Theorem is why summed grain contributions tend Gaussian. Test it on the sound: at every sample, the output is the sum of the grains sounding then. Count how many sound at once, and how close the spread of sample values comes to a bell curve. A bell curve has kurtosis 3; a spikier, heavier-tailed spread scores higher.
