The timbre, and where the window lives

Until now every voice was a sine: one partial each. Here every voice becomes a sawtooth with its own tone filter that follows its own octave. The illusion survives, as long as two things are wired right: the filter follows the voice, not the note, and the bright window weighs each voice, not each overtone.

Four things to try

  1. The build that works. Press endless up with the defaults. Each voice is a sawtooth whose filter sits four times above its own pitch. It sounds like an instrument now, not a test tone, and it still never arrives. Over 90 % of the energy is on the played note in some octave.
  2. Turn the brightness up. Push the ratio toward 16. The tone opens, and the fifth (blue) grows, because the third overtone of every voice is a fifth above it. At 16 the note holds about 84 % and the fifth about 9 %. The note blurs a little but doesn't break: every voice adds the same fifth, so it sounds like color, not a second pitch.
  3. Make the wiring mistake. Switch to "the played note." Now every voice's filter follows one shared signal that folds back once per octave. Watch the brightness line: once an octave it drops by a fifth of an octave in an instant. The pitch is still seamless, but the tone has a seam.
  4. Move the window to the sum. Set the ratio to 16, then put the window on the summed audio, which is how the page first drew it. Voices far below hearing now get in through their overtones. The wrapping voice arrives at about −30 dB, a thump once an octave, and the lowest voices flutter like a motor. Widen the stack to 15: the thump drops to about −64 dB, but the flutter stays. The fix is to put the window back on each voice.

The math, twice

a_k,h = (1/h) · L(h·f_k ; r·f_k) · W(x_k) L(f ; fc) = 1 / √(1 + (f/fc)⁴)
(level of overtone h of voice k) = (sawtooth weight 1 ÷ h) × (tone filter at that overtone, cutoff = ratio × voice's pitch) × (window at the voice's octave)
L(h·f_k ; r·f_k) = 1 / √(1 + (h/r)⁴) → the same for every voice, at every pitch
(tone filter at overtone h) = 1 ÷ √(1 + (overtone number ÷ ratio)⁴), which doesn't depend on the pitch at all
window on the sum: W(x_k + log₂ h) instead of W(x_k)
(window on the sum) weighs each overtone by its own octave, (window on the voice) by the voice's fundamental. Overtone h sits log₂ h octaves above its voice: 5 octaves for the 32nd.

The second line is why ratio tracking is safe. When the cutoff is a fixed multiple of the voice's own pitch, every voice has the same shape of tone, so climbing an octave turns each voice into exactly what its upper neighbour was. Tracking the played note breaks that, because the cutoff folds back an octave when the note does, while the voices don't.
