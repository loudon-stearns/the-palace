SMPTE LTC · biphase mark code · four steps

Time, written as a square wave

A timecode becomes eighty bits, the bits become flips of a level, and the flips are audio. Four steps: one bit; one word of eighty; the waveform the word makes; and last, a reader that is told nothing reading the audio back. Nothing plays until you press a play button.

Eight cells · click a cell to flip its bit. Dotted lines are cell boundaries; the level flips at every one, and once more in the middle of a 1.
One word · the eighty bits of the timecode in step 2 as the level they make, sync word at the right. Above each cell, its bit.
The rule, before the run Fifty frames from step 2's timecode are encoded at each rate and played through a plain audio path: a 10 Hz high-pass, as a coupling capacitor would, and noise 30 dB under the peak. The reader is told nothing: not the rate, not the polarity, not where a cell starts. It times the gaps between zero crossings and keeps its own guess of the bit period, and it names the rate from how far apart its sync words come and from the drop-frame flag. It assumes the tape is played at its own speed: a 25 fps tape played 1.2× fast would be named 30, and no reader can tell the two apart from the audio alone. Pass: at every one of the six rates, and with the polarity flipped, all 50 frames come back with their own timecodes, no wrong reads, the bits in order, and the rate named right. Fail: any frame missing or any wrong read. The control: the same bits sent as plain levels, high for 1 and low for 0, with no clock in them, through the same reader. It must read nothing. If it read the frames too, this test would say nothing about self-clocking. It is a weak control: a reader built for biphase fails on plain levels almost by construction, so it shows where the clock lives more than it tests the reader. Chance: a reader guessing bits would need 80 right in a row for one frame; it would read none.
