Portamento by Ear

single-session reel · ~20 minutes · 6 clips + 3 pairs

Six glides. For each: listen twice, name the regime in your head (overdamped · smooth arrival, no overshoot  |  critically damped · fastest no-overshoot landing  |  underdamped · overshoots and rings back), then guess the physical system. Reveal. Move on. The first round is unambiguous; the second sharpens the ear. The third moves the ear off the pitch line and onto the tone colour: a bowed string tells you how it arrived in its timbre too.

Round one — clear signatures
01
A slow brass glide. Which regime?
Reveal
Overdamped
Trombone — heavy slide, long air column, brass damps overshoot.
Smooth exponential approach. No "thump" of arrival, no ring. The slide's mass and the air column's loss together push ζ well above 1.
02
A synth glide that lands fast and clean.
Reveal
Critically damped
Synthesizer — engineered glide circuit tuned for the fastest no-overshoot arrival.
The shortest path that does not ring. ζ=1 is an engineered target; circuits like this are built to it.
03
A bell struck slightly off, settling toward its true pitch.
Reveal
Underdamped
Bell — light strike that detunes the mode; resonance rings around the target.
The pitch swings past the target and oscillates around it while decaying — the textbook ring-back. ζ well below 1.
Round two — sharper edges
04
A pitch sneaks up through the air. No mechanical contact.
Reveal
Overdamped
Theremin — capacitive coupling, no mechanical inertia, glide rate set by hand motion.
No instrument-side dynamics to overshoot; the hand is the damping. Almost everything from a theremin sounds this way.
05
An acoustic guitar bend, released with discipline.
Reveal
Critically damped
Guitar — bend released with discipline, string tension settles fast.
Tempting to call underdamped — guitar bends often ring — but a controlled release brings tension home with no swing past. The finger is doing the damping work.
06
An electric guitar — fast bend, then finger vibrato.
Reveal
Underdamped
Electric guitar — fast bend, string tension overshoots, finger relaxes back.
Speed is what makes this underdamped: a slow bend would let the player ride the tension home; a fast one outruns the correction and overshoots. Vibrato keeps the ringing visible.
Round three — hear the arrival in the tone colour

A bowed string holds its overtones onto exact multiples of the note. When an underdamped glide overshoots, one overtone (here the fifth partial) can't keep up: it goes about 2% out of tune, beats a few times, and catches again as the pitch settles. Listen for a brief roughness or flutter high in the tone just after the pitch crests. Each pair is the same bow on the same string, G3 up to E4.

Bowed string — B's player lands with a fast, loose shift and swings past the note.
You can hear B's pitch swing past E4, and that is enough to answer. Now listen again for the second cue: around one second in, as B's pitch crests, the top of the tone flutters for half a second, then again more briefly on the next swing. That is the fifth partial slipping out of tune and catching. A never passes the note, so nothing comes loose.

Both follow the same underdamped pitch path, sample for sample.
In B the fifth partial has been held in tune by force, so the only difference between the two clips is the slip. If you heard A flutter, you heard the overshoot in the timbre alone, with the pitch giving you nothing to go on. That is the skill this round is for. If they sounded the same, try headphones and listen to the upper half of the tone at about one second in.

Neither overshoots. Can the tone colour tell these two apart?

Reveal
A · overdamped B · critically damped
Bowed string — A eases in, B arrives clean and fast.
No. Neither glide passes the note, so no partial slips and both tones stay whole. The timbre cue separates underdamped from the other two, and it stops there. To split overdamped from critical you go back to the shape of the approach: B gets within a semitone of the note about 0.6 s after it starts moving, while A takes more than twice as long (1.3 s) and is still a quarter-tone flat at 1.7 s. Knowing where a cue stops working is part of having the ear.
If you got 5 or 6 right — you have the regime ear. Next session: take the paired exercises to drill the physical-system guess.

If you got 3 or 4 right — re-listen to the misses with the reveal open. The overdamped/critically-damped boundary is the hardest: both lack ring, and only the shape of the approach separates them.

If you got 0–2 right — start over with eyes on the reveals. Knowing the answer before listening trains the regime onto the sound; surprise comes back after two passes.

Round three — pair 08 is the one that counts. If you heard the flutter there, you can hear an overshoot without following the pitch at all, which is how you will catch it in a dense mix.
