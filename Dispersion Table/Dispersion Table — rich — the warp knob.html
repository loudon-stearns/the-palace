The warp knob

The page names the practical engine: second-order allpasses, spread across the band in log steps, with one pole radius for the warp. It says that one knob goes from pure, through piano, to bell. Take one allpass alone first, then the eight together, then put them in a string, and last sweep the knob and measure what it does.

1 · One allpass: same level, different time

An allpass passes every frequency at exactly the level it came in with. What it changes is when. Near its centre frequency it holds the sound back, and the pole radius sets how long and how narrowly. A click through it comes out as the same click, smeared in time.

doPlay the clicks. Switch the allpass in and out, then turn up the pole radius.
watchThe level stays a flat line at every radius. The delay grows a peak at 1 kHz. The click grows a ringing tail.
hearAt low radius, nothing changes. Near 1, each click rings at 1 kHz: a ping, with nothing taken away.

2 · Eight of them, spread in log steps

The page's recipe: eight sections, centres from 300 Hz to 12 kHz in equal musical steps, all on one pole radius. Their delays add. At radius 0 each is just two samples of delay, the same at every frequency. As the radius rises the low frequencies are held back more than the high ones, with a bump at each centre. That curve, how long each frequency takes to get through, is what a dispersion table would store.

doPlay the clicks. Sweep the pole radius.
watchThe delay curve tilting and growing its eight bumps. The click spreading out into a falling sweep.
hearThe click turns into a short zap, the sound a spring reverb makes, still at full level.

(the cascade's lag) is the sum of the eight lags, and (how long each frequency takes to get through) is how fast that lag grows with frequency

3 · In the string

Now put the eight in a Karplus-Strong loop at 110 Hz: a delay, the average, and the allpasses. The bare delay is shortened by their low-frequency delay, so the first partial stays near 110 Hz. Every partial lands where its whole trip, delay plus average plus allpasses, comes to a whole number of turns.

doPluck at radius 0. Then raise it a little at a time, plucking as you go.

4 · The test: pure, piano, bell?

Sweep the knob from 0 to 0.97 and sort what comes out. Pure: every partial within a cent of a whole multiple. Piano: the partials stretch sharp, more for each one up, and follow the piano string's law fₙ = n f₀ √(1 + B n²) to within a tenth of their spread. Past that, a stretch the law no longer fits. And irregular: some partials flat while others go sharp, no longer a stretched string at all. That is where the page puts its bell, but it is a new shape, not a wider spread: the widest departure at the top of the knob is about what the piano band already reaches at its top. Then try the other common recipe, eight identical first-order allpasses, the stiff string's.
