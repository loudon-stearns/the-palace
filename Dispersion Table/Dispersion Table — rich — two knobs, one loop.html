Dispersion Table · from one curve to a table · four steps

Two knobs, one loop

The page's table has two axes: one picks the loop filter's magnitude, how bright the string is and how fast it dies, and the other its phase, where the partials land. Take each knob alone, then the two as a table you can move across, and last ask whether they really stay apart.

1 · The magnitude knob alone

A loop with a gentle lowpass in it: three taps, g, 1 − 2g, g. Its level falls toward the top of the band, so on every trip the high partials lose more than the low ones and the pluck goes from bright to dark. Its phase is a straight line, one sample at every frequency, and that sample is taken out of the bare delay.

2 · The phase knob alone

Now hold the brightness and turn the warp: the page's eight second-order allpasses, from 300 Hz to 12 kHz, on one pole radius. Their level is one at every frequency, so on any one trip they take nothing. What they move is where the partials sit.

doPluck at no warp, then at more.
watchThe bottom chart: the partials stretching off the grid. The top chart: whether how fast they die stays put.

3 · Both, as a table

Put the two knobs at right angles and you have the page's table: across, the magnitude; up, the phase. Any point on it is one loop filter. Click anywhere to pluck the string that point makes.

4 · The test: do the axes stay apart?

If the two axes are truly apart, sweeping the magnitude knob end to end should move no partial, and sweeping the phase knob should change no decay. Both sweeps run from the point you last chose, at both pitches, with the three-tap filter and with the brightness control most strings use instead: a one-pole lowpass, set to darken the eighth partial by exactly as much.
