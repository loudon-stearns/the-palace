# Outside read — 2026-10-19 — Adopt the Craft, Author the Seam

Workshop file for the morning. Not canon.

## What it claims, in two sentences

When an AI joins an old craft, take the craft off the shelf whole and build new things only at the seams, where a collaborator who read loosely is replaced by one that needs exact input. You know you've found the right seam when one vocabulary does three jobs at once: the record's schema, the machine's conditioning, and the format for handing work between stages.

## Where it would teach badly

1. **"The machine requires exactness" is half true.** A diffusion model does read loosely: it reads a prompt *more* loosely than a human would. What it does with that looseness is fall back to its own default (the centred front-on portrait, in Blocked, Not Prompted's words). So the seam is where a reader with *taste* is replaced by a reader with *defaults*. Exact input is the price of overriding the defaults. That's sharper than "exactness" and explains why the seam costs so much: you're authoring everything the human used to supply from taste.
2. **"Seam" is drifting.** Here it means a reader-swap. In Comic and Cinema it means a boundary between two registers. A learner meeting both would think they're the same claim.
3. **"Only that point is new work" overclaims.** Cheap rendering changes the upstream craft too: you board differently when a render costs a minute. The third-seam question half-knows this.
4. **No test outside BLUELINE.** Stated as a generalization, it lands on one case.

## The piece

**Hand the Groove to a Machine** (`hand-the-groove-to-a-machine.html`). This tests the claim in Loudon's own craft, music, which the page names and never works.

A lead sheet says *swing, laid back*. A drummer reads that loosely and plays it with feel. Swap the drummer for a machine and play the same chart: it comes back straight and on the grid, because the machine has nothing in it that reads "swing". That's the centred portrait in sound. Then you author the seam, in two numbers the drummer never needed: the swing ratio (where the off-beat eighth lands) and the lay-back (how many milliseconds behind the grid the backbeat sits). When the machine with those two numbers comes close to the drummer, the page is right. Where it can't (the drummer's push into the turnaround, the ghost notes), that's either a third seam or the honest limit of the claim.

It also tests "one vocabulary, three jobs": those two numbers are the chart's schema, the machine's conditioning, and the format any other tool could read. A second row shows the same swap for BLUELINE's board→render seam in words, so the music isn't on its own.

Prior art: BLUELINE's `proofs/seam-a-roundtrip/` already tests seam 1 (staging vocabulary survives 2D→3D, 17 PASS / 3 WARN / 0 FAIL). That's the visual seam's proof, so this rough doesn't redo it.
