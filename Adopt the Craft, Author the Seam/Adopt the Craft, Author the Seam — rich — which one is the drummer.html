Which one is the drummer?

The page says its move works in any craft: the seam is where a collaborator who interpreted is replaced by one who requires exactness. Here is the craft it names and never works, music. A chart goes to a drummer, then to a machine; you author the seam; then you find out, blind, whether what you authored carries the groove.

The chart, read by a drummer

Medium swing · laid back · 96 bpm · | Dm7 | G7 | · ride, hat on 2 and 4, feathered kick, walking bass

This is the craft taken off the shelf whole: a lead sheet, a ride pattern, a walking bass, and two words of feel. A drummer reads swing, laid back loosely and supplies the rest from taste. The skip note on the ride lands late, near a triplet. The ride and hat sit a hair behind the bass. Two and four lean. No hit lands exactly where the last one did.

Coral dots are the drummer's hits; grey are the bass and the feathered kick, which stay on the grid. This drummer is a model, not a person: swing 64%, the ride and hat 22 ms behind, every hit wandering up to 7 ms and 15% in weight, as rough two had it. As the chart asks, it plays only the chart's notes, so what you hear is feel. With rough two's leftovers it also drops a ghost note on the snare (the third lane) after 3 now and then, and a pickup into the turnaround: notes nobody wrote down, and no number in the record below has a field for them.

The same chart, read by a machine

Hand the same chart to a sequencer. Nothing in it reads swing or laid back, so it falls back on its default: straight eighths, dead on the grid, every hit the same weight. Nothing is wrong with what it plays. It just isn't the tune.

Amber dots are the machine's. Watch the skip note: the machine's sits on the dashed line, half the beat; the drummer's sits near the dotted one, two thirds. This is the seam, in the page's words: a collaborator who interpreted replaced by one who requires exactness. (The page's outside read puts it more sharply: a reader with defaults in place of a reader with taste.) Everything the drummer supplied is now missing.

One number: where the skip note lands

The first thing to author is the thing the drummer never had to be told. Swing is one number: how far into the beat the skip note falls. 50% is straight, 67% a true triplet, 75% a dotted eighth and sixteenth.

t(skip) = t(beat) + s × T
(when the skip note lands) = (when its beat starts) + (the swing ratio) × (the length of one beat, 0.625 s at 96 bpm)

Two numbers and a switch: the seam, authored

The second number is where the ride and hat sit against the bass, in milliseconds behind the grid. The switch is whether two and four lean. That is the whole record: the drummer's taste written down as two numbers and a switch. Set them yourself, or take the drummer's.

t(hit) = t(grid) + L
(when the ride or hat is struck) = (when the grid says) + (the lay-back)

With the drummer's numbers taken, the amber dots sit where the coral ones average. What the record has no field for is the wander: no two of the drummer's bars are alike, and the machine's are.

The test: which one is the drummer?

Twenty-four trials. Each plays two takes of the same two bars, one after the other. One of them is the drummer. Say which.

Three kinds, eight of each, shuffled together; you aren't told which kind a trial is until the end.

The control: the drummer against the machine reading the chart as written. The machine's straightness is built in here, so this tests your ear, not the page: if you can't tell these apart, the other two kinds can't speak.

The wander: the drummer as the chart asks, against the machine reading the drummer's own seam (64%, 22 ms back, leaning on 2 and 4). By construction this drummer IS that seam plus a wander an agent sized: about 3 ms in timing and 0.7 dB in weight, on average. So this kind asks one thing: is that wander heard?

The leftovers: the drummer with rough two's ghost notes and turnaround pickup, against the same seam. This is the kind that can go against the page's two-number record: 7 or more says the drummer played something you hear that the record has no field for, so the seam is wider than two numbers and a switch.

Chance is 4 of 8 in each kind. A coin gets 7 or more of 8 in 9 runs out of 256, 3.5%. Because the drummer comes first in exactly four trials of each kind (said below), a non-listener who answers "first" five times in a kind does a little better: at most 4 in 56, 7.1%.

The rule, fixed now: in any kind, 7 or 8 of 8 means you can tell the drummer from that machine. 6 or fewer, the test didn't show that you can, which is not the same as showing there was nothing to hear.

Nothing else tells them apart. The trials differ in nothing but the takes: every take's drums are set to the same loudness (the same RMS over the take), and the bass is the same in both. Each pair has the same length. The drummer comes first in four trials of each kind and second in the other four, shuffled. The grid stays dark. Every trial draws a fresh drummer.
