Can a trail be too strong?

The open question: can a deeply worn path foreclose lateral exploration, because workers always follow the same route? The page already records the palace's answer, on Swarm Weave § The oblique lens. This piece does not re-answer it for the palace. It gives the question a number in the ant model the page borrows: a colony worn onto one source, a better one opened later, and the trail strength at which the colony can no longer leave.

    A colony on one source, nothing else on the table

    A hundred ants, one source 40 units away. They wear a trail. How strong it gets is set by how much each trip lays, q, against the model's evaporation, ρ = 0.01. Move q and watch where the trail settles.

    settled trail ≈ q · (ants ÷ round trip) ÷ ρ = q · (100 ÷ 80) ÷ 0.01 = 125 q
    The trail settles where what evaporates each tick equals what is laid: the ants' trips per tick times q. A round trip is 80 ticks here, 40 out and 40 back, so a hundred ants make 1.25 trips a tick, and the trail settles near 125 q, wobbling a few percent as trips come home.
    tick 0trail 0trail ÷ k (k = 20) 0

    k = 20 is the trail it takes before a trail matters to an ant (the choice rule from the cluster piece). Keep an eye on trail ÷ k: the test turns on it.

    A fresh colony, two sources at once

    Before asking whether a worn colony can leave, check that this colony can find the better source at all. Two sources open from the start, both bare: the far one at 40 units and a near one at 15. Same food at both; the only difference is distance.

    tick 0far share, last 100 ticks –near share –

    An ant on the near path gets home every 30 ticks, one on the far path every 80, so the near path is reinforced more often per ant, and the loop from the cluster piece does the rest. Drop q to 0.1 and the trails never pass k: choices stay close to a coin toss and the colony can't tell the near source is better.

    The worn colony meets a better source

    Now the order changes. Only the far source is open for 2000 ticks, long enough for its trail to settle. Then the near source opens, bare. Same colony, same rule, same food. Play, and see whether the ants come over.

    tick 0far trail 0near trail 0near share, last 100 ticks –

    Outside the palace, this is the shape of the double-bridge experiments of Goss, Aron, Deneubourg and Pasteels (Naturwissenschaften, 1989), where a shorter branch offered after the longer one had been in use is the classic case of a colony that does not switch. Their numbers are not checked here; this model's are.

    The test: at what strength does the colony stop being able to leave?

    The worn colony and the fresh one, side by side, at eight trail strengths set by q. Twenty seeded runs each. A worn colony may take a long time to leave, so it is given a long time: 40,000 ticks after the near source opens, fifty times the far round trip. The rules were written before the first run, and the run takes some seconds.

    the rules, stated first
    • Chance level: an even split, 0.50 of choices on each source.
    • A strength counts only if a fresh colony finds the near source there: near share at least 0.50 in ticks 3500 to 4000, in at least 18 of 20 runs. Below that the trail is too faint to choose by, and the row is marked so.
    • The horizon: 40,000 ticks after the near source opens. A worn run has left if, in any 500-tick window before the horizon, the near source takes at least 0.50 of the choices.
    • In a counted row, the worn colony leaves if at least 18 of 20 runs have left by the horizon; is locked if at most 2 of 20; otherwise split.
    • A trail cannot be too strong, in this model at this horizon, if every counted row reads leaves. It can be if any counted row reads locked; the answer is the weakest such trail. Locked means not left by 40,000 ticks, not never.
    • The off-trail arm, scored by the same rules: the worn colony with one ant in ten choosing a source at random, the model's own chance cover.
    • Held fixed: colony size, distances, food, n = 2, k = 20, ρ = 0.01, the seeds. Only q, and so the trail's strength, changes along the rows.

    Whose numbers these are: this model's, at its k, n, ρ, colony size and horizon. The column left by 4,000 is there to show why the horizon is long: read that early, colonies that leave later look locked. The palace's answer to the question stands where the page puts it, on Swarm Weave: a weave cuts the map once more with no axis at all. That is a cover cut across a map, not ants wandering off a path, and this model does not judge it. The echo worth keeping is Kuramoto Coupling's: too little coupling and nothing cooperates, too much and one voice dominates.
