Does a trail no one walks fade?

The section says trails that are never reinforced fade. This piece runs that sentence on the same colony engine as the cluster piece above, and scores the fade itself: once a trail's source runs out, does its strength fall away, and by when? The answer turns on what counts as reinforcing. Four steps; the test is the last.

    A trail with nobody on it

    One path, a trail laid down to τ = 100, and no ants at all. Nothing adds to it. Every tick it keeps all but a fraction ρ of itself.

    τ(t) = τ0 · (1 − ρ)t    half gone after ln 2 ÷ −ln(1 − ρ) ticks
    The trail after t ticks is what it started with, times the share it keeps each tick, t times over. It is half gone after about 0.69 ÷ ρ ticks: 69 ticks at this model's ρ = 0.01.
    tick 0trail τ 100half gone at tick –the formula says –

    This step is the model's rule, not a test: a trail with nobody on it fades here because the code says so. Set ρ to zero and it never fades at all. Hold that: a trail fades here only while evaporation takes more than anything lays.

    A colony settled on a strong trail

    A hundred ants, two paths of the same length, food on both. Path A starts strong, as in the cluster piece, and the colony settles on it. Let it run, then empty A's food when you like and watch what the colony does.

    tick 0trail A 0trail B 0A's share of choices, last 100 ticks –

    An ant that walks to an empty source comes home with nothing and lays nothing. Here an ant marks only when it brings food home, so emptying A stops A's reinforcement at once, though ants keep walking it for a while.

    When walking a path marks it

    Change one thing: an ant now lays part of its mark on the way out, whether or not there is food at the end, and the rest only when it comes home with food. A trip that finds food still lays 1 in all, so the settled trail is as strong as before. But now an ant walking a dead path still marks it. Empty A and watch what the trail does.

    each walk out: τ ← τ + qout    each trip home with food: τ ← τ + qhome    qout + qhome = 1
    Part of the mark is laid just for walking the path, the rest only for success. Whether and how real ants split their marking is outside this piece; the split here is this model's setting.
    tick 0trail A 0trail B 0A's share of choices, last 100 ticks –

    Set qout to 0 and this is the last step again. Raise it and a dead trail that ants still walk keeps part of itself; whether it ever falls is the test.

    The test: does a dead trail fade, and by when?

    The settled colony, run 2000 ticks with A strong, then A's food is gone. The trail on A is then followed for 40,000 ticks. Three ways of marking, and a null with A's food left in place. Twenty seeded runs each. The rules were written before the first run.

    the rules, stated first
    • Fades: A's trail falls below a tenth of its strength at the moment its source went. Read at two horizons, both fixed before the run: 1,000 ticks after, and 40,000.
    • At a horizon, the sentence holds for an arm if at least 18 of 20 runs have faded; fails if at most 2 of 20; otherwise it is split.
    • The arms: marks only coming home with food (qout = 0); half going out (qout = 0.5); most going out (qout = 0.7). Pure evaporation predicts a tenth by tick 230, so an arm that has not faded by 1,000 is being held up by something.
    • The null: A's food left in place, marking only coming home. The same measure must not read faded; if 3 or more of 20 do, the measure is broken and the piece says so.
    • Held fixed: colony size, path lengths, n = 2, k = 20, ρ = 0.01, the head start, the seeds, and 1 laid per successful trip. Only where the mark is laid changes between arms.

    Whose numbers these are: this model's, at its rate and its marking rule. They show what the sentence needs in a colony: a trail fades when nothing lays on it, and walking a path counts as laying if walking marks.
