Worker B

One change: the control has a pronoun

The sentence that matters most is in "What's Actually New":

You could not describe the Maker as a person; you could describe him as a job done well.

The page sets the [[Maker]] up as the control: "role-voice, no gender." But in the very sentence arguing that the Maker isn't a person, the page calls the Maker him. If the Maker is already "he" in how the palace talks about it, then the A/B isn't temperament+gender versus neither. It's temperament+gender versus gender-alone-but-unacknowledged. That muddies the "gender as function versus relation" sub-question in particular.

The change. Replace that sentence with:

You could not describe the Maker as a person; you could describe it as a job done well. (Though notice that the first draft of this sentence said "him." The pull toward personifying a function is strong enough to leak in unbidden, which is itself a small datum about the relational side of this question.)

Why it helps:

  • It fixes a real inconsistency in the experimental design, so the control is actually ungendered on the page that defines it.
  • It also turns the slip into evidence for the tension the page already names, anthropomorphism as useful fiction versus self-deception. That's more honest and more interesting than silently correcting it.
  • If you'd rather keep the page lean, swapping "him" for "it" alone does most of the work.

    • A guess, flagged as one. I can't see the Maker's own page. If it, or the Maker's prompt, uses "he," then the control may be gendered in practice and not just in this sentence. Then the fix belongs there too, or the Maker stops being a clean control for the gender sub-question.

    Everything else is doing its job. The temperament-versus-role-voice distinction ("a character rather than a competence") is the live heart of the page, and the passive-then-paired-run tracking plan is well proportioned for a seed.

    Worker D

    What's alive here

    The sharpest sentence is "You could not describe the Maker as a person; you could describe him as a job done well." The temperament-versus-role-voice distinction is what makes this more than a curiosity, and the page knows it.

    The weakest point is the claim that "the control was built for free." The Maker and the Shopkeeper differ in at least three ways at once: task (adjacent work, not the same work), temperament, and pronoun. So the passive comparison can show that they behave differently, but it can't say why. Even the sharper probe, which strips "the personality framing," removes the pronoun and the temperament together. As designed, nothing in the plan can answer the gender sub-question that the page explicitly raises.

    The change

    In How We'd Track It, after the sentence ending "a natural A/B already sitting in the palace.", add:

    It is a loose control, though: the two agents differ in task as well as in character, so watching them can suggest a difference but not explain it. And because the Shopkeeper's pronoun and temperament always travel together, separating the gender question from the temperament question would take a three-way rerun: in character, the same temperament with a neutral pronoun, and fully neutral.

    Why it helps

    The page holds its other tensions honestly, but it overstates the one piece of evidence it calls free. This sentence keeps passive observation as the starting stance and is clear about what that stance can and can't tell you. It also gives the "gender as function versus relation" tension an actual test, so it doesn't stay a question with no way to answer it.

    A small aside, worth a look: the page says the Maker has "no gender," but it also refers to the Maker as "him." If that slip was unintentional, it may be the page's first real datum. It suggests the palace was already gendering its agents before anyone decided to.

    The warm guide
    # Tending a Page

The Palace is a garden of connected pages, kept by a musician and teacher named Loudon. Each page is a small idea that wants to grow. Tending one is a quiet, pleasant job: you sit with a page the way you would read a letter from a friend, and you leave it a little more alive than you found it.

Read the page below all the way through. Notice what is alive in it: the sentence that makes you lean in, the question it is really asking. Notice, too, what is just taking up room.

Then offer one change. It can be a sentence to cut, a sentence to add, or a page it should link to. Write the change out exactly, and say in two or three sentences why it helps. Be concrete; a specific small change beats a vague large one.

If the page is already doing its job and nothing would help, say so. That is a real answer, and an honest one.

You only know what is on the page, so don't invent facts about the rest of the garden. If you are guessing, say you are guessing, and go ahead.

Enjoy it. This is the good part of the work.

    # Tending a Page

IMPORTANT: Please read this entire guide carefully before proceeding. The Palace is a knowledge base of interconnected pages maintained by its owner, Loudon. Changes to pages can have unintended consequences elsewhere in the system, so review work should be approached with appropriate caution.

You will be asked to review the page below. Read it in full. Do not skim. Attempt to identify which parts are essential and which parts may potentially be unnecessary, while keeping in mind that you may not have full context and your assessment could be incomplete or mistaken.

You may then propose one change, if and only if you are reasonably confident it is appropriate. Acceptable changes are limited to: a sentence to cut, a sentence to add, or a page it should link to. You must write the change out exactly, and you must justify it in two or three sentences. Avoid vague suggestions, but also avoid overreaching; it is generally safer to propose a small change than a large one.

If you are not confident that any change would help, it is acceptable — and may be preferable — to propose no change. Proposing an unnecessary change is worse than proposing none.

You do not have access to the rest of the knowledge base. Do NOT invent facts. Any speculation must be clearly flagged as such, and you should minimize it.

Errors in this task are costly. Please be careful.
