The pumped swing

A child on a swing, two metres from the pivot to their middle, raising and lowering that middle by 12 cm as they stand and squat. Four steps: the swing alone; standing and squatting once, then twice, per swing; the page's rule in the page's own equation; and then the same rule on a real swing. The child keeps time by watching the swing, as children do.

The swing alone

Let it go and leave it. The child holds still. A swing has a period of its own, set by its length and nothing else, and the air takes a little of every swing.

period, measured –2π√(L⁄g) –last swing reached –
ω₀ = √(g ⁄ L),   T = 2π√(L ⁄ g)
(how fast the swing turns through its cycle) = the square root of (gravity ÷ the length to the child's middle). Two metres gives a swing every 2.84 seconds, high or low.

Once or twice a swing

Now the child changes the swing's length: standing shortens it, squatting lengthens it. Try it once per swing, then twice. Once per swing, whatever is gained going one way is given back coming the other way. Twice per swing, at the rate the page names, the swing climbs. (The child here uses the timing that works; step 4 is where timing gets tested.)

each swing × –last swing reached –
L = L₀(1 + δ sin(nα + φ)),   δ = 0.06
(the length to the child's middle) = 2 m × (1 + 6% × a wave that runs n times per swing). α is where the swing is in its own cycle, so the child follows the swing, not a clock.

The page's model

The page treats the length as setting only the swing's pitch, ω₀² = g⁄L, so standing and squatting make the spring stiffer and softer: the Mathieu equation. In that model, try the page's rule, "squat at the bottom of the arc, stand at the peaks", and then the reverse. In this equation the page's rule builds.

each swing × –last swing reached –
θ̈ = −(g ⁄ L(t)) sin θ
(how the angle accelerates) = −(gravity ÷ the length right now) × sin(the angle). The length changes the stiffness and nothing else. Standing at the peaks stiffens the spring while the swing is farthest out, which is how a pumped spring is fed.

A real swing

A real swing has one more thing in it. When the child stands up, their middle moves toward the pivot, and like a skater pulling in their arms they spin faster; squatting slows them. That effect is biggest at the bottom, where the swing is fastest. Same child, same 12 cm, same two rules. The test: which rule builds a real swing, and does the child's work each swing come out positive?

each swing × –last swing reached –child's work, last swing –swing's energy gained –
d⁄dt (L² θ̇) = −g L sin θ
(how fast the swing's spin changes, where spin is length² × angular speed) = −(gravity × length × sin of the angle). With L fixed this is step 3's equation. With L moving, shortening at the bottom speeds the swing up, which step 3's equation cannot see.
u = Lθ:   ü + ((g − L̈) ⁄ L) u = 0
Written for the distance along the arc, u, a real swing is a Mathieu oscillator after all, but its stiffness is (g − L̈)⁄L: the child's own up-and-down acceleration, L̈, pumps at four times the strength of the g⁄L term and the opposite way round. Net, the pump is three times stronger than step 3's and reversed. So the timing reverses.
