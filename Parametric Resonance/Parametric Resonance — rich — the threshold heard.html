The threshold, heard

One resonator at 220 Hz, and a pump that wobbles its stiffness at 440 Hz. Four steps: a ring alone, a pump too weak to win, a pump strong enough to win, and then the page's open question, a driven ring and a pumped one swept side by side. Each step plays from a click. You can jump to any of them.

One ring

Strike the resonator once and leave it alone. Nothing pumps it. Watch the fade in decibels: it is a straight line, because the damping takes the same fraction of what is left on every cycle.

ẍ + 2ζω₀ẋ + ω₀²x = 0,   fade rate = ζω₀
(how the ring's position accelerates) + (a drag that grows with its speed) + (a spring pulling it home) = nothing pushing it. Here ω₀ = 2π × 220 Hz and ζ = 0.0015, so the ring loses ζω₀ = 2.07 nepers a second: 18.0 dB a second.

A gentle pump

Now make the spring stiffer and softer by a fraction ε, at 440 Hz, twice the ring's own rate. The pump adds nothing to the sound directly. It multiplies the ring instead of pushing it, so you never hear 440 Hz; you hear what it does to the 220. Below the threshold, the ring still fades, only slower.

predicted fade –measured –rings – as long as unpumped
ẍ + 2ζω₀ẋ + ω₀²(1 + ε cos 2ω₀t) x = 0,   fade rate ≈ ω₀(ζ − ε⁄4)
The same ring, but its spring's stiffness rises and falls by a fraction ε, twice per cycle. The slowest way to fade is now the drag minus a quarter of the pump depth, both measured in cycles of ω₀. When ε⁄4 catches up with ζ, nothing is left to fade.

Across the threshold

Start from silence. The only thing in the resonator is a faint noise floor, 80 dB down, the room's own hiss. Set a depth and hold it, or sweep it. Below εc the floor stays a floor. Past it, the pump wins: whatever tiny ring the noise left grows by the same fraction every cycle, until the pump runs out of strength. Try heavy damping too: the threshold moves.

εc = 4ζ,   growth rate ≈ ω₀(ε⁄4 − ζ)
The pump wins when a quarter of its depth is more than the damping, so the threshold depth is four times the damping ratio: a lightly damped ring tips over at a shallow pump, a heavily damped one needs a deep one. The predicted rate printed here is the exact one, from the Floquet multipliers of one pump period; the formula above is its first-order form.

Driven or pumped

The page asks whether pumping has its own sound. Here is the test it proposes. Drive the same resonator with a force that sweeps up to 220 Hz in six seconds, the familiar way into resonance. Then pump it with a depth that sweeps up through εc in the same six seconds. Both start from the same quiet. Both traces stay on the plot so you can compare them.

Driven: a force from outside, on the right-hand side; the ring's size follows the force, so it is there the whole way and grows as Ω nears ω₀. Pumped: the right-hand side is zero; the pump only scales the spring, so it can grow only what is already ringing, and from a noise floor that takes time. "Audible" here means above the dotted line, 60 dB down on the plot.
