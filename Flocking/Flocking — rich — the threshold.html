Where is the threshold?

The section says flocking is Kuramoto in two dimensions: the flock's polarization is Kuramoto's R, and Kuramoto's critical coupling becomes a regime of density and radius where coherent flow appears. Here a heading becomes a phase, R is built from the phases, and then you go looking for the threshold: first with nothing for alignment to overcome, then with a spread.

Step 1 of 4 · one idea

A bird's heading is a point on a circle, the same thing as an oscillator's phase

Six birds, alignment only. A bird flying at an angle θ is, as far as alignment cares, an oscillator at phase θ: a point on a circle. On the right each bird is a dot at its heading. Alignment pulls each dot toward the others it can reach, as Kuramoto's coupling pulls each phase toward the crowd's. The plane is what Kuramoto lacks: it decides who can reach whom, and that changes as the birds move.

θi turns toward arg Σj near i vj, at most a small force each frame
Alignment: (bird i's heading) turns toward (the heading of its neighbours' summed velocity), by no more than a fixed nudge a frame, and only the birds in reach count.
Step 2 of 4 · the order parameter

R for headings dealt at random: a floor, not zero

Eighty headings dealt at random, no flock at all. Add them as arrows of length one and take the length of the sum per bird: that is R, Kuramoto's order parameter and the flock's polarization, the same formula. Random headings don't give 0. They give a small floor that shrinks as the crowd grows, about 0.1 for eighty. Any R a flock shows has to be read against it.

Step 3 of 4 · coupling alone

With nothing to overcome, any alignment orders the flock, only slower

The bundle's flock, eighty birds from seed 7, with alignment as the coupling on a slider. Kuramoto has a threshold because something pulls its oscillators apart: their natural speeds differ, or noise jostles them. Below it, coupling can't hold them together. These birds are identical and nothing knocks them off course, so alignment has nothing to overcome. Turn it down and the flock still orders; it just takes longer, and below about 0.05 too long to tell from never. The Plot lens on this page reads its alignment sweep a short while in; the dashed line is fifteen seconds, where its report read it.

Step 4 of 4 · the test

Add noise and a threshold appears that moves with the noise, the density and the reach

Now every frame each bird's heading takes a random twist, up to ±η half-turns. It is fresh every frame, not a fixed difference between birds: the twin of identical Kuramoto oscillators driven by noise of strength D, which lock only above Kc = 2D. It gives alignment something to overcome. Pick the noise, how many birds share the field, and how far each can see, then move alignment and wait. Your dot is R averaged from ten seconds after your last change. The curves are the same flocks measured headless: four starts, a minute to settle, a minute measured. The threshold marked is where R crosses 0.5.
