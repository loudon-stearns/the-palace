# Auditory Illusions

Every one of these is the hearing system caught doing its normal job in a place where the normal job gives the wrong answer. That is why they are worth collecting. A sound you hear that isn't in the air marks the stage of hearing that made it up, and tells you what that stage assumes about the world. Lay them out in order and they climb the ear, from the air to the meaning, and out into time and silence, each one a probe of its stage.

Strictly, an illusion is a real sound heard wrong, and a hallucination is a sound heard with nothing there. The page holds both, and its last section ends in the true hallucinations. Almost everywhere else in the palace *hallucination* names the failure the house guards against, a model making things up; here it is also how hearing works. Both are true. All hearing is a construction; an illusion is the moment the construction shows.

Most of them have a twin in the eye, and each entry names it. The twins are not equally strong. Some share a **mechanism** — the same kind of neural trick, run on light and on sound. Some share only a **shape** — the same structure, built by different machinery. Where nothing honest fits, the entry says so; the missing twins are a map too.

## In the air — the baseline

**Beats.** Play 440 Hz and 443 Hz together and you hear one tone that swells and fades three times a second. This one is not you: the two waves really do drift in and out of phase, and the pulse is in the waveform. It is the control for everything below, the line between what physics does and what you do. It is also two oscillators that never lock, the picture [[Kuramoto Coupling]] starts from.
*Seen:* moiré — two fine gratings laid over each other make broad bands that are really in the image. Mechanism: both are interference, a slow difference pattern riding on two fast ones.

## In the cochlea — the ear is not a microphone

**Combination tones.** Play two loud tones, 1000 and 1200 Hz, and a third, fainter tone appears at 800 Hz (2f₁ − f₂), sometimes a low one at 200 Hz (f₂ − f₁). Neither is in the air. Tartini heard them on the violin in the eighteenth century. The cochlea makes them: its outer hair cells amplify sound and, like any amplifier pushed hard, distort it. What it proves: the inner ear is active and nonlinear.
*Seen:* no clean twin. The eye has no amplifier in front of it that distorts this way.

**Masking.** A loud tone hides a quiet one close to it in frequency, but not one far away. What it proves: the cochlea is a bank of overlapping bandpass filters, the critical bands, and two sounds in the same filter compete. The MP3 is built on this; it throws away what your filters were going to hide anyway.
*Seen:* lateral inhibition, the reason a grey square looks darker on white than on black. Shape: one signal hides or shifts a neighbour. The cochlea's version is swamping inside one filter; the eye's is usually explained as neighbours suppressing each other.

## Between the two ears — timing to tens of microseconds

**Binaural beats.** Put 440 Hz in the left ear and 443 Hz in the right, on headphones, and you hear a beat at 3 Hz that exists in neither ear. The waves never meet in the air; they meet in the brainstem, which compares their timing (Dove, 1839). It fades above about 1500 Hz, where the nerve can no longer follow the wave's phase. What it proves: the brain compares the two ears' timing to within a few hundredths of a millisecond. The claims that binaural beats entrain brainwaves are another matter and remain weakly supported.
*Seen:* the Pulfrich effect — dim one eye with a filter, and a pendulum swinging flat seems to swing in an ellipse, because the dim eye's signal arrives late. Shape: a timing difference between two sensors becomes a percept neither has.

**Huggins pitch.** Each ear gets white noise, and alone each sounds like nothing but hiss. Together a faint melody appears. The noise is identical in both ears except in one narrow band, where its phase is twisted between them (Cramer and Huggins, 1958). A pitch made only out of the difference between two ears.
*Seen:* Julesz's random-dot stereogram — each eye sees random dots, and a shape floats out in depth only when both look. Mechanism, and the tightest pair on the page: researchers call dichotic pitch the random-dot stereogram's auditory analogue.

**The precedence effect.** The same sound from two speakers, a few milliseconds apart, is heard only from the first. What it proves: the brain takes the first arrival as the source and suppresses the echoes after it, which is how you can place a voice in a room full of reflections.
*Seen:* no clean twin. Light arrives too fast for echoes to matter to the eye.

**The Franssen effect.** A tone starts in one speaker and is crossfaded to a second. Listeners go on hearing it at the first, after that speaker has gone silent. Location is decided at the onset and then held.
*Seen:* no clean twin.

## Pitch is computed, not measured

**The missing fundamental.** Play 200, 300 and 400 Hz together and you hear 100 Hz, a pitch with no energy at it. A phone line cuts everything below about 300 Hz, yet a low voice still sounds low. What it proves: pitch is not a place on the cochlea but a pattern the brain reads, the period the harmonics share. Small speakers use it to fake bass they cannot play. Ohm and Seebeck argued over it in the 1840s, and it took a century to settle on Seebeck's side.
*Seen:* the Kanizsa triangle — three notched discs, and you see the bright edges of a triangle that isn't drawn. Shape: a structure perceived exactly where there is nothing, implied by what surrounds it.

**The Shepard tone.** One pitch class stacked across every octave, inside a loudness window that hides the top and bottom: it climbs forever and arrives nowhere (Shepard, 1964; Risset made it glide continuously in 1969). What it proves: pitch has two parts. *Chroma* goes round a circle (C, C♯, D … C); *height* goes up a line. The Shepard tone deletes the height and leaves only the circle. The [[Shepard Tone Synthesizer]] builds it stage by stage, and its later stages found where it breaks: the stack fuses only while every voice is exactly an octave from its neighbour, the glide must be one glide for the whole stack, and the loudness window has to sit on each voice, not on the mix.
*Seen:* the barber pole, and the Penrose stair Escher drew as *Ascending and Descending* (1960). Shape, and a famous one: Shepard and E. E. Zajac's 1967 Bell Labs film ran the tone over an animated Penrose stair. Shepard worked both sides — in 1990 he published the Shepard tables, two identical tabletops that look nothing alike, and wrote that understanding the illusion is "virtually powerless" against it. The tone is the same: knowing how it works does not stop it climbing.

**The tritone paradox.** Two Shepard tones a tritone apart, C then F♯ — exactly half the circle, so neither way round is shorter. Some listeners hear the pair go up, others hear it go down, and each listener is consistent (Deutsch, 1986). The split tracks where people grew up: in Deutsch's 1991 study, listeners raised in California and in southern England tended to hear the same pairs in opposite directions, and Deutsch traced the split to the pitch range of the speech they grew up hearing. What it proves: which way pitch moves is partly learned.
*Seen:* the dress (2015) — one photo, blue-and-black to some and white-and-gold to others, each person sure. Shape: a stable split between people, set by their assumptions. Not the Necker cube, which flips inside one viewer; this one doesn't flip.

**The octave illusion.** On headphones, 400 and 800 Hz alternate, always opposite between the ears. Most people hear a high tone in one ear alternating with a low tone in the other, a pattern that was never played (Deutsch, 1974). What it proves, on Deutsch's account: *what* the pitch is and *where* it is are worked out by separate systems, and they can be glued together wrongly. Others explain it differently, and the account is still argued.
*Seen:* illusory conjunctions — flash a red X and a blue O and people report a red O. Mechanism, if Deutsch is right: features computed apart, bound wrong.

## Sorting the world into sources

**Auditory streaming.** A high and a low tone in a galloping pattern, ta-ta-ta, ta-ta-ta. Slow and close in pitch, it is one rhythm. Faster or further apart, it splits into two lines and the gallop is gone. What it proves: before anything else, the brain sorts sound into sources, by rules — close in pitch, close in time, same timbre (Bregman, *Auditory Scene Analysis*, 1990).
*Seen:* Gestalt grouping by proximity, and apparent motion. Mechanism, arguably: Bregman showed the auditory scene follows the grouping laws first found for vision, and a 2001 study found streaming, apparent motion and static dot displays group the same way. Remez counters that shared rules describe the grouping without explaining it.

**The scale illusion.** A rising and a falling scale, with alternate notes swapped between the ears. Nobody hears the zigzag in each ear; most hear a smooth melody in one and another in the other (Deutsch, 1975). Grouping by pitch beats grouping by place.
*Seen:* grouping by similarity — dots of two colours read as rows of colour, not their real arrangement. Shape.

**The continuity illusion.** Cut a gap in a tone and fill it with a burst of noise, and you hear the tone go on through the noise. Do it to a syllable and you hear the missing sound: phonemic restoration (Warren, 1970). What it proves: when something covers a sound, the brain fills in what was probably there.
*Seen:* amodal completion — the cat behind a picket fence is seen as one cat. Mechanism, and Bregman's own pairing: the noise is the fence, and the gap is in time instead of space.

**The gap transfer illusion.** A long glide with a short gap in it crosses a short glide with none. Listeners hear the long one as continuous and the gap as belonging to the short one (Nakajima and colleagues; found in 1992, published in 2000). The brain builds events out of onsets and endings, and here it pairs them wrong.
*Seen:* no clean twin.

## Meaning gets in early

**The McGurk effect.** Watch a face say "ga" over audio of "ba" and you hear "da" (McGurk and MacDonald, 1976). Shut your eyes and it goes back to "ba." Hearing speech is already seeing.
*Seen:* the sound-induced flash illusion, McGurk turned round — one flash with two beeps is seen as two flashes (Shams, Kamitani and Shimojo, 2000). Mirror pair: the eye rewrites the ear in one, the ear rewrites the eye in the other.

**Yanny or Laurel.** One recording (2018), heard as one name or the other, each listener sure. The cues for "Yanny" sit higher; people who weight high frequencies, or have younger ears, or better speakers, hear it, and others hear "Laurel."
*Seen:* the dress again — called the audio dress at the time, and rightly.

**Phantom words.** Two words looped and overlapped from two speakers. After a while people hear words that aren't there, and the words they hear often have to do with what is on their minds (Deutsch).
*Seen:* pareidolia — faces in clouds, in electrical sockets. Shape: the pattern-finder supplies the pattern.
*Machined:* [[Whisper]] does a cousin of it. Give the transcriber a long silence and it can write "thank you for watching," having decided the gap means the video is over. Its input is absent where Deutsch's is ambiguous, but the move is the same: a system built to find words supplies them from what it expects when the input is too thin to hold them.

**Speech to song.** Loop a spoken phrase — Deutsch's was "sometimes behave so strangely," in 1995 — and after a few repetitions it is being sung. Go back to the whole sentence and the phrase still sings. What it proves: the line between talking and music is drawn by the listener, and repetition moves it.
*Seen:* the hidden Dalmatian, or any Mooney image — a blotch that becomes a dog and can't go back to blotch. Shape: one-way recognition.

## In time

**The Risset rhythm.** The Shepard tone moved into the time domain: a beat that speeds up forever, by fading between copies at half and double tempo. Same window, same circle, now around tempo. Tempo is a rate on the same axis as pitch ([[Frequency-Time Duality]]), so this is the Shepard loop run on the slow side of it.
*Seen:* the infinite zoom — a picture that contains itself, zoomed into forever. Shape.

**Rhythm turns into pitch.** Speed up a click. Below about 20 a second you hear a fast tapping; somewhere past 30 to 50 it has become a buzz with a pitch, with a muddy zone between where neither sense is sharp. Nothing about the sound changes kind; you do. Stockhausen's essay *…wie die Zeit vergeht…* (1957) argued this — rhythm and pitch as one axis of vibration, cut in two by perception. See [[Frequency-Time Duality]]. [[Boundary-Crossing Instruments]] takes this as its first case: one dial, several perceptual worlds along it.
*Seen:* flicker fusion — a light flashing fast enough looks steady, which is why a projector's light doesn't seem to flicker. Shape: a rate past which the sense stops counting events. The ear goes further and turns the rate into a pitch; the eye only sees it steady.

## Hearing what isn't there

**The spectral motion aftereffect.** Listen for a few minutes to a sound pattern drifting upward in frequency, then play it standing still. It seems to drift down (Shu and others, 1993).
*Seen:* the waterfall illusion — stare at falling water, look at the rocks, and they rise. Mechanism: tired motion detectors, in either sense.

**The Zwicker tone.** Listen to broad noise with a band cut out of it, then stop the noise. For a few seconds you hear a faint tone, pitched inside the missing band, in silence (Zwicker, 1964). Between about half and most people hear it, depending on the noise. The best guess is that neurons around the gap were holding each other down, and when the noise stops they overshoot; the mechanism is still argued.
*Seen:* the negative afterimage — stare at red, look at white, see green. Zwicker named it after that, "negative afterimage in hearing." Shape for certain; mechanism likely different, since vision's version is fatigue and this one may not be.

**Tinnitus and musical ear syndrome.** When input drops away — hearing loss, a very quiet room — the auditory system turns its gain up and starts hearing its own noise: a ringing, sometimes music. Most people who hear the music know it isn't real. These are the true hallucinations, and the Zwicker tone is the lab's small, safe version of them.
*Seen:* Charles Bonnet syndrome — people losing their sight who see vivid figures and patterns, and know they aren't there. Mechanism: clinicians call musical ear syndrome auditory Charles Bonnet. A sense deprived of input fills the hole.

The twinless rows cluster. That is a guess from this page's list, not yet tested against the eye's (Context, open question 3). They are the ones about the cochlea's own distortion and about arrival time — the things only an ear does, because sound is slow and the ear is a pressure sensor working at the speed of the wave. Run the other way and the eye's twinless illusions are about geometry and size, the Shepard tables among them. Hearing builds its world in time; seeing builds it in space; where a trick belongs to one of those alone, it has no twin.

## What the climb teaches

Line the stages up and each one is a bet about the world: the first arrival is the real one, harmonics that share a period belong to one thing, a sound covered for a moment is still there, a face and a voice say the same word. They are good bets, usually right. An illusion is where the bet shows. Helmholtz's name for it, unconscious inference, still fits: hearing is less a recording than a fast, layered guess about what is out there.

Two things stay true at once. Most of these survive the explanation — know exactly how the Shepard tone works, and it still climbs. Yet some move with the listener: the tritone paradox follows where you grew up, phantom words follow what is on your mind, and speech to song can't be undone once heard. Part of the guess is built in and can't be argued with; part of it is you.

That is why the page wants to become a set of instruments. Nearly every one here can be built, and building it is how you find where it breaks, which is where the physics and the perception come apart. The Shepard tone is the first, and the most built.
