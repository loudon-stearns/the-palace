Two ways to morph a string

An additive tone at A2, 110 Hz, its partials placed by one stiff string. We start on the flat harmonic line and end on the section's claim: that morphing the string's one number stays a real string all the way, and blending the two end curves point by point does not. Silent until you press a button.

The flat line

This is the curve the Wavetable B editor draws: one dot per partial, its height how many cents that partial sits above the harmonic n × 110 Hz. With B at zero every dot is on the flat line and the tone is harmonic. Raise B and the line bows upward: the low partials barely move, the high ones run sharp. One number moves all twenty-four.

fn = n · f0 · √(1 + B · n²)    cn = 600 · log2(1 + B · n²)
(the nth partial) = (the partial number) × (the fundamental) × the square root of (one plus the stiffness times the partial number squared); drawn as (cents above the harmonic) = 600 × the base-two log of (one plus the stiffness times the partial number squared).

Read the stiffness back

A string has one stiffness, so every one of its partials, read backwards through the formula, gives the same B. That is the test of whether a curve is a string: read B off each partial and see whether the line is flat. Here the curve is a string at B = 0.002. Drag any dot up or down with the pointer and its partial reads a different B: the curve is no longer one string. The second readout finds the nearest real string to whatever you drew, letting both B and the fundamental move, and says how far off its worst partial is. Step 5 uses both.

Bn = (2cn/600 − 1) / n²
(the stiffness partial n reports) = (two to the power of its cents over 600, minus one) ÷ (the partial number squared). A real string reports the same number from every partial.

Undrawn, the curve is step 1's at B = 0.002.

Morph the number

Two strings, the section's own pair: string A at B = 0.001 and string B at B = 0.003. The first way to morph is the one the section recommends: slide the stiffness itself from one to the other. At every point of the slider there is one B, so every point is a string you could build. The lower panel stays flat the whole way; it only rises.

B(t) = (1 − t) · BA + t · BB
(the stiffness at morph position t) = (one minus t) × (string A's stiffness) + t × (string B's stiffness).

t = 0 is step 1 at B = 0.001; t = 1 is step 1 at B = 0.003.

Blend the curves

The second way is what scanning between two frames of Wavetable B does: take the two curves as they are drawn and mix them partial by partial, so each dot moves straight from its height on A to its height on B. The ends are the same two strings. In between, read the stiffness back: the line tilts. Partial 1 reports the average of the two stiffnesses; the higher the partial, the less it reports, sliding toward their geometric mean, which is lower. A string whose stiffness depends on which partial you ask is not one string. Switch between the two morphs at the same t and listen for it.

cn(t) = (1 − t) · cA,n + t · cB,n    ⇒   1 + Bnn² = (1 + BAn²)1−t (1 + BBn²)t
(partial n's cents at t) = (one minus t) × (its cents on A) + t × (its cents on B), which means the stiffness it reports is a weighted average at the bottom of the spectrum and slides toward a weighted geometric mean at the top.

At t = 0 and t = 1 the two morphs are the same string; step 3 is the other morph.

Is the blend a string?

The section says the blend's in-between states may be no physical model, while the number's are always a string. Here every point of both morphs is measured against the nearest real string, the step 2 readout run at every t, generous to the blend: B and the fundamental are both free to fit it. The number's morph is a string by construction and reads zero; the question is how far the blend strays, and what sets it. Move the two strings apart and add partials, and watch it grow.
